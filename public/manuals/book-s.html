§ 1 Principles

This book describes how the Railway speaks to its drivers from the lineside: with lights, with boards and, on the platform, with a disc held in a hand. It was written from first principles rather than inherited, and it tries to say everything once.

The Fernhollow branch. This seventh edition opens the Railway's first junction. At the north end of Wending the line now divides: straight on to Coldwater, or over the Fernhollow branch into the side valley. A junction is a switch that chooses between lines, and everything in this edition that is new follows from that: a home signal for each line converging on it, routes that hold the junction for one movement at a time (§ 7.6), a subsidiary under two home signals that calls a train on to an occupied platform so that two cars can be joined (§ 2.4), and trains that arrive as one and leave as two (§ 7.7). Nothing that the earlier editions said has changed its meaning.

The Coldwater extension. The sixth edition was the largest since the first. The line now runs beyond Wending to Coldwater at the head of the valley, Wending has become a through station with a platform on each loop track, and two trains may be on the line at once. That has brought distant signals, subsidiaries under two starting signals, two block sections worked by Line Warrants, a neutral section with its boards, and a whistle board for a farm crossing. Everything the earlier editions said about a signal's meaning is still true; what has grown is the number of places where it is said.

A signal is a promise. The box promises that the road is yours; you promise to go no further than the promise.Notice pinned above the frame, Wending Box

1.1 Eight principles

Everything in this book follows from eight decisions taken when the Railway was laid out. Where a later chapter seems arbitrary, one of these will explain it.

  1. One meaning per aspect. An aspect means the same thing at every signal on the Railway. There are no local variations, no special cases marked by a plate, and no aspect whose meaning depends on the one before it.
  2. Everything fails to STOP. A lamp that goes out, a wire that breaks, a lever that jams: each of them produces a signal at STOP, or a signal that must be read as STOP. Nothing that can fail can fail towards "go".
  3. A signal faces the trains it governs. A signal speaks only to the trains coming towards its face, and to no others. A driver never has to work out whether a signal is meant for them; if they can read it, it is.
  4. Two kinds of movement, two kinds of signal. A train movement runs from station to station under main signals. A shunting movement shifts vehicles within a station under ground signals. The two kinds of signal look nothing alike. A main signal speaks to every movement; a ground signal speaks only to shunting.
  5. Fixed things are boards; changing things are lights. A board tells you something that is always true at that place: stop here, slow to this, shunt no further. A light tells you something that is true now. You never have to wonder whether a board might have changed.
  6. Every signal has a name you can say on the telephone. A station code and a number. Odd numbers face Down, even numbers face Up. Nothing else on the Railway is named like a signal.
  7. The signal is the box's voice; the baton is the station master's hand. To go, a passenger train needs both, where both exist. To stop, it needs only one.
  8. The signal stands where you stop. The position of a signal is the stopping place for a train it holds. There is no allowance for overlap to remember and no distance to estimate; the front of the train stops at the post.

To these the Coldwater extension added a ninth, which belongs with the other eight: one warrant, one train, one section. A train enters the single line between two stations only holding that section's Line Warrant, of which there is exactly one; while it holds it, nothing else can (§ 7.4).

The Fernhollow branch added a tenth: one movement over a junction at a time. A junction is a switch that chooses between lines, and every movement over it is one route, which holds the switch from the clearing of its signal until the movement has passed clear (§ 7.6). Two trains may stand at Wending together; they may not both be at the junction.

Older railways accumulated their signalling one accident at a time, and their rulebooks show it: exceptions to exceptions, aspects that mean one thing on a branch and another on the main line. We had the rare good fortune of starting with a blank sheet and a valley to fill. We chose to spend that fortune on simplicity, because a simple rule is one that is obeyed at four in the morning in the rain.

1.2 Who speaks, who listens

The box
Each station has a signal box, named for the station: Ashgrove Box, Wending Box, Coldwater Box and Fernhollow Box. The box holds the frame that sets switches and clears signals, and the register in which the Line Warrants are entered. At every station the station master works the box in person.
The station master
At Ashgrove, T. Marrow; at Wending, J. Pell; at Coldwater, A. Ashby; at Fernhollow, M. Thorne. The station master sets routes, holds the baton, exchanges line clear with the neighbouring boxes, and is the authority for every movement within station limits. Messages from the box are signed "Marrow, Ashgrove", "Pell, Wending", "Ashby, Coldwater" or "Thorne, Fernhollow". Wending Box, with a box on each of three sides, exchanges line clear with all three.
The driver
Obeys every signal and board that faces them, obeys the baton, and drives no faster than the boards allow. Where the driver is in doubt, the driver stops. Book D describes the driver's work in full.
The Signal Engineer
Owns this book, the signals, and the frame. Nobody else alters a signal or its meaning.

Directions are as defined in Book R: Down is the direction of increasing kilometrage, from Ashgrove through Wending towards Coldwater; Up is the direction of decreasing kilometrage. Kilometrage counts from the buffer stop at Ashgrove, km 0.000, to the buffer stop at Coldwater, km 6.400. Wending lies between, km 2.850 to 3.500 within its limits. The Fernhollow branch has a kilometrage of its own, counted from the junction switch WD J (main km 3.360) to the Fernhollow buffer stop at branch km 2.320, and written with the letter F: F 0.8. On the branch, Down is away from Wending.

§ 2 Main signals

Main signals govern every movement that reaches them, train or shunting. They are colour lights on a slate post, with three lamps in a vertical row and a black plate carrying the signal's name. They show the same thing by day and by night.

2.1 The head

The three lamps are always in the same order: green at the top, amber in the middle, red at the bottom. Only one lamp is lit at a time. The head is mounted on a slate post at a height where the driver sees it over the whole approach, and the plate beneath the head gives the signal's name in white on black.

2.2 The aspects

AspectShowsMeaning

CAUTIONamber, middle lampProceed; be prepared to stop at the next signal, stop board or end of track.
CLEARgreen, top lampProceed at line speed; the next signal shows a proceed aspect.
(dark, doubtful)nothing, or more than one lampTreat as STOP.
Rule S 10Signals at STOP

Never pass a main signal at STOP, and never pass a ground signal at SHUNT STOP. A main signal at STOP whose subsidiary is lit (§ 2.4) is STOP for a train and SHUNT for a shunting movement. Bring the front of the train to a stand at the post and wait for the aspect to change or for the box to speak. Passing a signal at STOP is a signal passed at danger and is written in the Incident Book whatever the outcome.

Rule S 16Dark or doubtful signals

A main signal that shows no light, or more than one light, or a light you cannot read with confidence, is at STOP. Stop at it and speak to the box (§ 9).

CAUTION and CLEAR are both proceed aspects: they give you the road as far as the next signal. They differ only in what they tell you about what lies beyond. CLEAR promises that the next signal is also showing a proceed aspect, so line speed is safe. CAUTION promises nothing beyond the next stopping place, so you approach it able to stop.

The platform convention. A home signal that admits a train to a platform track where the train will stop shows CAUTION when cleared, never CLEAR. At a terminus there is no next signal, only the stop board and the end of the track; at Wending the next signal is the starting signal at the far end of the platform, which is at STOP until the train has done its station work. Either way the aspect says honestly that the next stopping place is close. So AG 2, WD 1, WD 8 and CW 1 never show green for a stopping train. A train booked to run through Wending without stopping would be admitted under CLEAR once its starter had been cleared; none is yet booked.

Three aspects are enough for the Railway we have, and we resisted adding a fourth. Every extra aspect is one more thing to distinguish through a wet windscreen, and one more way for a tired driver to be nearly right. When the line grew and a second train came onto it we did what we had said we would: we added distant signals that repeat a home signal's aspect from further back (§ 2.5), and invented no new colour.

2.3 Kinds of main signal

Main signals are all the same to look at; they are named for what they do.

Home signal
Stands on the single line at the approach to a station and governs entry. Ashgrove's home is AG 2; Wending has three, WD 1 from the Ashgrove side, WD 8 from the Coldwater side and WD 10 from the Fernhollow side; Coldwater's is CW 1; Fernhollow's is FH 1. WD 8 and WD 10 carry a subsidiary that can call a train on to an occupied platform (§ 2.4). A train held at a home signal stands outside station limits.
Starting signal
Stands at the end of a platform track and governs departure onto the single line. Ashgrove's starter is AG 1; Coldwater's is CW 2; Fernhollow's is FH 2; Wending, with two platform tracks and a single line at each end, has four: WD 2 and WD 4 towards Ashgrove from tracks 1 and 2, WD 7 and WD 9 northwards from tracks 1 and 2. A northward starter at Wending reads over the junction WD J, so it is the starter for Coldwater and for Fernhollow alike; the switch indicator at WD J and the box's message say which (§ 6.3). A starting signal is cleared only with the Line Warrant for the section ahead in the box's hand (§ 7.4).
Distant signal
Not a main signal but the repeater of one: it stands one warning distance before a home signal and tells the driver what the home is showing. It has two lamps, never shows STOP, and is named for its home with a D. § 2.5.

Every main signal carries a telephone to its box at the foot of the post. If you are stopped at a signal and the box has not spoken to you, use it.

2.4 Subsidiaries and the call-on

Four of Wending's main signals carry a subsidiary: the starting signals WD 4 on track 2 and WD 7 on track 1, and the home signals WD 8 and WD 10. At the starters it is a shunting indication; at the homes it is also a call-on, which is described after the figure. A subsidiary is a small black box beside the head with two white lamps, the same lamps as a ground signal. Normally they are dark. When the box clears a shunt route that begins at that signal, the main head stays at STOP and the two white lamps light diagonally, rising to the right: the SHUNT indication. It means exactly what SHUNT means on a ground signal (§ 3): proceed as a shunting movement, at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of anything, as far as the route goes. It means nothing at all to a train.

Why not a separate ground signal beside the starter, as at the other stations? Because the two would stand on the same post for the same track, facing the same way, and a driver reading two signals at one place is a driver who may read the wrong one. The subsidiary puts the shunting indication on the starter itself, where it is the only thing to read. At Wending the run-round leaves track 1 under WD 7's subsidiary towards the north stub, and leaves track 2 under WD 4's towards the south stub (§ 7.3). WD 3 of the earlier editions, which did the first of those jobs, has been withdrawn.

A lit subsidiary is still a main signal at STOP. A train standing at WD 7 with the white lamps lit has not been given the road, and departs only when the amber or the green shows and the baton is raised.

The call-on at WD 8 and WD 10

Two of Wending's home signals carry the same two white lamps, and there they mean one thing more. WD 8, the home from Coldwater, and WD 10, the home from Fernhollow, are marked for call-on. When the box has a train standing in the platform and a second train is to be coupled to it, the frame cannot clear the home to CAUTION, because the platform is occupied. Instead the main head stays at STOP and the subsidiary lights SHUNT for the arriving train. To that train it says: the platform ahead is occupied; you are called on; enter at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of the vehicles standing there, and couple to them only as the box has instructed you. The train becomes, for the length of the platform, a shunting movement, and the speed and the care of a shunting movement apply to it (Rule D 10 for the coupling itself).

Rule S 30Call-on

A home signal's subsidiary lit for a train is a call-on: the platform ahead is occupied. Pass at shunting speed prepared to stop short of the vehicles standing there, and couple to them only as the box has instructed. Only WD 8 and WD 10 are marked for call-on; at every other subsidiary a train is at STOP.

The box says what it is doing before the driver sees it: "Line clear for 1003 to Wending. Warrant C issued; WD 10 shows its subsidiary: call on to platform 2 and couple to 1002 standing there." A driver who sees the white lamps at WD 10 without having been told so treats them as a failure and stops (§ 9).

Joining two cars in a platform is the whole purpose of the branch: a train from Ashgrove is split at Wending into a Coldwater portion and a Fernhollow portion, and on the way back the two portions are made one again. The second portion has to enter a platform that already holds the first. A ground signal could do it, but a ground signal cannot govern a train, and a train is what the second portion is, with its passengers aboard. So the home signal itself calls the train on, at a shunting movement's speed, and the box has told the driver whom to expect.

2.5 Distant signals

A distant signal stands one warning distance before a home signal and repeats what the home is showing, so that a driver running at line speed learns it early enough to stop at the home if the home is at STOP. It has two lamps on a slate post, green at the top, amber at the bottom, and behind the head an ivory chevron plate, point up, so that it can never be mistaken for a main signal even with its lamps out. It is named for the home it repeats with the letter D: WD 1D is the distant for WD 1.

Rule S 24Distant signals

At a distant signal at CAUTION, reduce speed so as to be able to stop at the home signal it repeats, whatever the home turns out to show. A distant at CLEAR promises a clear run through the station. A distant is never passed "at danger": it gives warning, not instruction, and the instruction is the home's.

A distant shows CLEAR only when its home shows CLEAR. Since every home on the Railway admits its trains to a platform where they stop, and therefore shows CAUTION at best (§ 2.2), every distant on the Railway shows CAUTION to every train that is booked to stop. This is not a fault and not a waste: the distant at CAUTION is telling the truth, that the driver must be ready to stop at the home, and the home at CAUTION then tells them they may come in. The day a train is booked through Wending without stopping, WD 1D will show green to it and mean it.

The distant stands one warning distance before its home, and the warning distance is the one the advance speed boards use (§ 4.2): 200 m where the approach to the home is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more. So AG 2D stands at km 0.670, 250 m before AG 2, because an Up train comes down the 12‰ grade to it; WD 1D at 2.680 and CW 1D at 5.780 stand 200 m before their homes on rising and level approaches; WD 8D at 3.640 stands 200 m before WD 8, because the 8‰ fall from Coldwater is under the 10‰ that earns the longer distance; WD 10D at F 0.330 stands 250 m before WD 10, because the branch falls at 10‰ to it; FH 1D at F 1.830 stands 200 m before FH 1 on the climb. Each distant stands within a few metres of the advance speed board for the same station, and the two are read together: the triangle says slow to 25, the chevron says be ready to stop.

On the shuttle a driver could see the home from the advance speed board and needed no repeater. With two trains on the line a home may be at STOP because the other train is still coming, and a driver who learns that only at the home has 145 m too little. The distant moves the news back to where the braking starts. We put it on a chevron plate and gave it no red lamp so that nobody, seeing amber on a plate, would ever stop there and wait for it to change: it will not, and the road behind them belongs to the block.

§ 3 Ground signals

Ground signals govern shunting movements: the short, slow moves within a station by which a locomotive runs round its train or vehicles are put where they are wanted. They are low, black and unmistakable. Shunting movements obey main signals as well; ground signals are the signals that belong to them alone.

A ground signal is a small black box on a short post, close to the ground, with two white lamps. It is placed at the point where a shunting move begins or must stop, and carries a name plate like a main signal. It shows one of two things.

IndicationShowsMeaning
SHUNT STOPtwo white lamps, horizontalDo not pass. Stand at the post.
SHUNTtwo white lamps, diagonal rising to the rightProceed as a shunting movement, at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of any vehicle or obstruction, as far as the route goes.
(dark)nothingTreat as SHUNT STOP.

3.1 What a SHUNT indication promises

A SHUNT indication promises less than a main signal does. It tells you that the switches on your route are set and locked, and that the box wants you to move. It does not tell you that the track ahead is empty. Very often it is not: the last move of a run-round is made under a ground signal into a platform track where a coach is standing, so that the locomotive can couple to it. That is why the indication carries its own speed and its own posture.

  • Shunting speed is 15 km/h (Rule R 10). It is a maximum, not a target.
  • Prepared to stop short means driving at a speed from which you can stop within the distance you can see to be clear. Approaching a vehicle to couple, that means 5 km/h over the last 20 m and contact at 2 km/h or less (Rule D 10).
  • As far as the route goes means to the next signal in your direction, to a Limit of Shunt board, or to the end of the track, whichever comes first.

3.2 Ground signals speak only to shunting movements

A ground signal is addressed to shunting movements and to nothing else. A train movement that has been given a proceed aspect at a main signal passes any ground signal within its route without regard to it; the main signal has already spoken for the whole route, and the ground signals along it stay at SHUNT STOP because nobody is shunting. So a Down train entering Wending under WD 1 at CAUTION passes WD 5 at SHUNT STOP on the stub without stopping, and has done nothing wrong.

This is not an exception to Rule S 10. The ground signal was never speaking to you. It is, though, the one place where a driver must know which kind of movement they are making, and the answer is always the same: if your last signal was a main signal's amber or green, you are a train; if it was a ground signal or a subsidiary, you are shunting. The other way round is stricter: a shunting movement obeys every main signal it reaches, and passes one at STOP only under its lit subsidiary (§ 2.4). A locomotive running round at Wending leaves track 1 under WD 7's subsidiary, not past WD 7 as if it were not there.

We could have arranged for the box to clear every ground signal along a train's route as well as the main signal, and some railways do. It costs levers and it costs time, and it teaches drivers to expect the white lamps to move for them. We would rather they read the black box as what it is: a signal for a different job.

3.3 Switch indicators

Every switch on the Railway carries a switch indicator at its toe: a black box on a short post, standing on the side of the line away from the diverging track, with a white bar on each of its two faces. The bar lies the way the switch lies. Along the track, the switch is set for the straight route, which on this line is always track 1 (normal). Leaning towards the diverging track, it is set for the diverging route, track 2 (reverse). The box has a face towards the toe and a face towards the branches, so it can be read from every leg of the switch.

Read it from wherever you are coming from. From the toe (a facing move) the bar tells you which route the switch will take you onto: straight into track 1, or leaning off into track 2. From a branch (a trailing move, as when you set back from the headshunt onto track 2 during a run-round) the same bar tells you whether the switch is set for the track you are on or against you: if the bar leans towards your track, or lies straight and your track is the straight one, the switch is for you; otherwise it is set against you, and the points would be forced open by your wheels if you went on. The cab's list of what lies ahead names the next switch and reads its indicator for you in the same words: Switch WD B · set for you, or SET AGAINST YOU.

A route's signal promises that the switches are set and locked, and the frame makes that promise hard to break. But a promise is worth more when it can be checked, and the driver is the one person who is actually at the switch. The indicator lets them confirm, in one glance, that the points lie as the signal said; on a trailing move it is the last check before the wheels reach the blades. Running through a switch set against the move bends the blades and their rodding, puts the switch out of use until the Engineer has been, and can derail the tail of the very move that did it. We would rather spend a lamp on every switch than a morning on any of that.

Rule S 22Switch indicators

Before moving over a switch, read its indicator. Never run through a switch that shows set against your move: stop and speak to the Box. Running through switches is an incident.

A switch indicator is not a signal. It carries no authority to move and none to stop: the signal governs the move, and Rule S 14 still applies in full. The indicator tells you how the switch lies, which is a different question, and one you should always be able to answer before your wheels reach the points.

4.1 Where boards stand

A stop board stands at the end of each platform in the direction of arrival, placed so that a train stopped at it has its doors along the platform. The Up stop board at Ashgrove is at km 0.135; the Down stop board at Coldwater at km 6.265. Wending, with a platform on each track, has two: the Down board on track 1 at km 3.165 for trains from Ashgrove, the Up board on track 2 at km 3.015 for trains from Coldwater. A stop board faces the trains that stop at it and is invisible, in effect, to trains leaving.

A Limit of Shunt board stands on the single line a short way outside each outer switch, facing the station: at Ashgrove at km 0.400 facing Down, at Coldwater at km 6.000 facing Up, and at Wending one at each end, km 2.900 facing Up and km 3.280 facing Down. The stretch between the outer switch and the board is called the stub, and it is exactly long enough for a locomotive to stand clear of the switch while it runs round. Wending has no headshunt; its north stub does that work.

Speed boards stand in pairs at the edge of station limits, back to back: a 25 facing the arriving train and a 50 facing the departing one. At Ashgrove the pair stands at km 0.450, at Wending at 2.850 and again at 3.400, at Coldwater at 5.950. Each 25 is announced by an advance speed board one warning distance out on the single line, and the warning distance depends on the grade of the approach (§ 4.2): 250 m at km 0.700 facing Up for Ashgrove, because an Up train comes down the 12‰ valley grade to it; 200 m at km 2.650 facing Down for Wending, up the last 6‰; 200 m at km 3.600 facing Up for Wending, down the 8‰ from Coldwater, which is under the 10‰ that earns the longer distance; 200 m at km 5.750 facing Down for Coldwater, on the level. Between the pairs, on the single line, the speed is 50 km/h; inside them, 25 km/h; shunting, 15 km/h, whatever the boards say.

Gradient posts stand at every change of grade, on the post side of the line: at km 0.500, where the level of Ashgrove station limits gives way to the 12‰ climb up the valley; at km 2.400, where the climb eases to 6‰; at km 2.800, where the line levels out for Wending; at km 3.400, where it climbs again at 8‰ towards the quarries; and at km 5.600, where it levels out for Coldwater. They are described in § 4.4, and the profile they mark is drawn in Book P.

The whistle boards for Millers' Crossing stand at km 4.500 facing Down and 4.900 facing Up, 200 m either side of the crossing at 4.700. The section boards for the neutral section stand at km 4.100 facing Down and 4.300 facing Up, and the resume boards at 4.300 facing Down and 4.100 facing Up; each pair is back to back on one post, section on the near face and resume on the far one. § 4.5.

A board is never lit and never changes. If a board seems to be wrong, missing or turned, do what the board would have said if it were there, report it to the box, and let the Signal Engineer sort it out.

4.2 Speed boards and advance speed boards

A speed board says what the speed is from where it stands. It is read against the whole train, not the cab: a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it. So a lower limit applies from the moment the front of the train reaches its board, and a higher limit applies only once the rear of the train has passed its board, because until then the tail is still on the slower stretch (Rule R 10). Leaving station limits, the driver waits for the last vehicle to clear the 50 board before notching up.

An advance speed board says that a lower limit lies ahead. It carries the numeral of the speed board it announces, on a triangle so that it cannot be mistaken for the board itself, and it stands one warning distance before that board. The warning distance is set by the grade of the approach: 200 m where the line up to the speed board is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more (Rule R 10). The driver must be at the announced speed when the front of the train reaches the speed board. Higher limits are not announced; there is nothing to prepare for. The distant signals use the same warning distance before their homes (§ 2.5), so at every station the triangle and the chevron stand close together on the approach.

We put the advance board 200 m out because a train at 50 km/h loses the 25 km/h down to 25 in about 145 m at a service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² on the level. On a 12‰ fall gravity takes back a quarter of that deceleration and the same braking needs about 190 m, so there the board stands 250 m out. What is left over in either case is the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the speed board itself is lost in fog or snow. On such a day the driver brakes at the advance board and does not wait to see the board it announces. Two distances, chosen by the grade, are still few enough for one habit: triangle, then brake.

Read with the posts: the Down advance board at 2.650 stands between posts 2.6 and 2.7 and its speed board at 2.850 between 2.8 and 2.9, with the last 6‰ of the climb between them; the Up advance board at 0.700 stands at post 0.7 and its speed board at 0.450 between 0.4 and 0.5, with the foot of the 12‰ fall and the gradient post at 0.5 between them. North of Wending the Up advance board at 3.600 stands at post 3.6 for the 25 board at 3.400, and the Down advance board at 5.750 between posts 5.7 and 5.8 for the Coldwater 25 board at 5.950.

4.3 Posts

Every hundred metres from the Ashgrove buffer stop to the end of the line at km 6.400 stands a hectometre post: an ivory plate on a slate post, carrying in black figures the distance from the origin in kilometres, a point, and the hectometre digit. The post at 1.4 km reads 1.4. At every full kilometre the plate is the same plate on a larger scale, rimmed in green, and reads the kilometre with its zero in the same black figures: the post at 2 km reads 2.0. A plate that reads like its neighbours is read at a glance; the green rim is all the distinction a full kilometre needs. The posts stand on the left of the line in the Down direction; within the loops they are moved outward past track 2 so that they stay clear of every track.

A post is not a signal. It tells you where you are, never what to do; a train never stops or slows for a post. Its work is to give every place on the line a name that everyone shares. The Wending stop board stands between posts 3.1 and 3.2; the Ashgrove home signal AG 2 stands at 0.42, between posts 0.4 and 0.5; a rail that needs looking at is "at 1.7, on the Down side". The tables in § 6 give every signal's kilometre, and every one of them can be read against the posts from the cab.

To quote a position, give the last post you passed and, if you know them, the metres beyond it: "stopped at 2.7, about 40 metres on". A Box hearing "2.7" knows at once which side of the Wending speed boards you are, and what to send. Drivers also use the posts to judge braking: from 50 km/h the step-2 application for a stop board begins about a post and a half before it (Book D).

4.4 Gradient posts

The Wend valley climbs. From the Ashgrove buffer stop the line is level through station limits, rises at 12‰ from km 0.500 to 2.400, eases to 6‰ as far as 2.800, and is level through Wending, which stands 25.2 m above Ashgrove. From km 3.400 it climbs again at 8‰ towards the quarries, and from 5.600 it is level into Coldwater, 42.8 m above Ashgrove. Down trains climb; Up trains descend. Every place where the grade changes is marked by a gradient post: a slate post carrying a small ivory disc at the top and two black arms, one reaching along the line each way. Each arm is tilted with the grade on its own side of the post and is lettered with that grade in per mille, or L where the line is level.

Read the post from wherever you are coming from: the arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. If that arm is tilted up and away from you, the line rises ahead; tilted down and away, it falls; level, it is level. The other arm tells you what you have just left. So the post at km 0.5 says to a Down train "level behind you, 12‰ up ahead", and to an Up train, reading the same arms from the other side, "12‰ down behind you, level ahead". The figures are the same from either side; only the sense of the tilt changes with the way you face, which is why the post needs no lettering for direction.

A gradient post is information, not instruction. Nothing about it tells a driver what speed to run or where to stop; it tells them what the line will do to the train, so that they can brake earlier on a fall, hold a train on a rise before releasing it, and know why the motors are working harder than the speedometer suggests. Book D gives the driving; Book P gives the profile the posts mark, drawn to scale.

Rule S 20Posts and gradient posts

Hectometre posts, kilometre posts and gradient posts are a position and profile reference and carry no instruction. Quote the posts when you report where a train, a fault or a person is; read the gradient posts to know what the line does ahead.

4.5 Whistle, section and resume boards

The Coldwater extension crosses a farm track and a boundary between two electrical feeds, and each of those has given the line a board.

The whistle board is an ivory disc with a black rim and a black W. It stands 200 m before a crossing and asks for one long blast of the horn as the train passes it (Rule R 18), so that anyone at the crossing hears the train before they can see it. The only crossing on the Railway is Millers' Crossing at km 4.700, a farm track between two of the mill's fields, guarded by gates the farmer keeps and by these two boards.

The section board and the resume board belong to the overhead line rather than to the track. Between km 4.190 and 4.210 the wire fed from Ashgrove ends and the wire fed from Coldwater begins, and between them is a short length that carries no current: the neutral section. A train coasts through it; a train that tries to draw current through it draws an arc at the pantograph and trips the substation. The section board, a black square with a white bar across the line, stands 90 m before the gap and says shut off power now; the resume board, the same square with the bar along the line, stands 90 m beyond and says power may be taken again. Both pairs face both ways, on shared posts at km 4.100 and 4.300, so that a Down train reads section at 4.100 and resume at 4.300 and an Up train reads the same posts in the other order. The driving is in Book D, Rule D 24, and the electrical reasons are in Book P.

None of these three boards has anything to do with the signals, and none of them changes. They are on the boards page because they are boards: fixed things that are always true where they stand.

The baton carries the human half of the decision. We could replace it with a lamp on a post and a button in the office, and one day, at a busier station, we may. 5.2 Where there is no baton

At an unstaffed halt there is no baton. The driver departs on their own authority when the doors are closed and the booked time has come. On the shuttle line worked under One Train Working (§ 8) this is the rule at both ends.

5.3 Other hand signals

Any person on the Railway may stop a train by any means that reads as "stop": a red flag, a red lamp, both arms raised, a light waved from side to side. A driver who sees such a thing stops, and asks afterwards. Nobody is ever wrong to stop a train.

§ 6 Station layouts and numbering

The two termini, Ashgrove and Coldwater, are laid out the same way, mirrored: a platform track and a loop, a switch at each end, a headshunt beyond the inner switch and a stub of the single line beyond the outer one. Wending, between them, is a through station: the same loop, but with a platform on each track and a stub of single line at each end. Here is every signal, board and switch, named and placed.

6.1 The numbering rule

Every signal is named by its station code and a number, written with a space: AG 2, WD 5. Station codes are two letters: Ashgrove AG, Wending WD, Coldwater CW, Fernhollow FH.

  • Odd numbers face Down (they govern trains moving towards increasing kilometrage). Even numbers face Up.
  • Main signals take the lowest numbers. At the termini, 1 and 2, and the ground signals take 3 to 6. At Wending, with three homes and four starters, the main signals are 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 9 and 10, and the ground signals 5 and 6. At Fernhollow, which has no loop, there are only FH 1 and FH 2.
  • A distant signal takes its home's number with the letter D: AG 2D, WD 1D, WD 8D, WD 10D, CW 1D, FH 1D. It faces the way its home faces.
  • Switches are named by station code and a letter: A is the switch at the Ashgrove end of the loop and B the switch at the Coldwater end; at a terminus one of them is the switch on the single-line side and the other the switch on the headshunt side. A switch is normal when set for track 1 and reverse when set for track 2. The junction switch at Wending is WD J: normal for the main line to Coldwater, reverse for the Fernhollow branch. Every switch carries a switch indicator at its toe, which shows the way it lies (§ 3.3).
  • The branch has its own kilometrage, counted from WD J and written with the letter F. A signal on the branch is placed by its branch kilometre: WD 10 stands at F 0.080, FH 1 at F 2.030. The letter belongs to the place, not to the signal; the branch's signals are named like every other.

So a driver who hears "AG 3" on the telephone knows at once that it is at Ashgrove, that it faces Down, and that it is a ground signal, before they have looked at a diagram; and "WD 8D" is at Wending, faces Up, and is the distant for WD 8.

§ 7 Routes and the box

A signal is never cleared on its own. It is cleared as the last act of setting a route, after the switches have been set and locked and the track has been shown to be clear. This chapter is about that sequence, and about the frame that enforces it.

7.1 What a route is

A route is a path through the station from one signal to the place where the movement will stop, together with the switch positions that make that path continuous. Every route has a governing signal: the one main signal or the one or two ground signals that the driver reads to make the movement. A route is set when the switches are in position and locked; it is cleared when the governing signal has been changed from STOP to a proceed aspect or from SHUNT STOP to SHUNT.

The frame in the box holds a lever for each switch and each signal. The levers are interlocked: a signal lever cannot be pulled unless the switches on its route are set the right way, and a switch lever cannot be moved while a signal reading over it is cleared. The frame is also wired to the track. Each switch, and each stretch of track between switches and signals, is a track section which reports whether a vehicle stands on it, and the frame will not clear a signal over an occupied section.

Rule S 14Moving on a route

Move only when the governing signal has been cleared for the move you are about to make, and only as far as the route goes: to the next signal facing you, to a Limit of Shunt board, to a stop board, or to the end of the track. When the movement is complete, stop and wait; the route will be taken back behind you and the next one set.

Two deliberate exceptions. A shunt route whose purpose is coupling is cleared into an occupied track. The frame permits this only for the routes marked "for coupling" in the tables below, only for a ground signal or a subsidiary, and only when the occupied section is the last one on the route. The driver approaches under Rule D 10. The second exception is the same thing done for a train: the call-on routes at WD 8 and WD 10, which admit a train to an occupied platform under the home's subsidiary so that two portions can be joined (§ 2.4, Rule S 30).

Once a route is cleared it is also locked: its switches cannot be moved, by that box or by any other route, until the movement it was set for has passed clear of them. § 7.6 says exactly when that is. At a station with one switch at each end this is scarcely noticed; at Wending, where three switches and a junction are shared by movements to three neighbours, it is what keeps the station honest.

7.3 A worked example: the run-round at Wending

  1. Arrival. WD 1D at km 2.680 shows CAUTION: be ready to stop at WD 1. Having begun to brake at the advance speed board at km 2.650, the train passes the 25 speed board at km 2.850 already down to 25 km/h and sees WD 1 at CAUTION: platform 1 is cleared for the train, and the next signal, WD 7, is at STOP. The train passes WD 5 at SHUNT STOP without regard to it (§ 3.2), runs through WD A set normal onto track 1, and stops with its front level with the Down WENDING stop board at km 3.165. The locomotive now stands at the Down end of the coach, facing WD 7 and the north stub. Wending Box cancels warrant A: the train is out of section.
  2. Station work. Doors open, passengers alight. The driver secures the locomotive, walks to the coupling, disconnects the brake pipe (both parts' brakes apply of their own accord) and parts the coupler (Rule D 12), then returns to the cab facing the north stub.
  3. Onto the north stub. Wending Box sets 1 → North stub: WD B is already normal; WD 7 stays at STOP and its subsidiary lights SHUNT (§ 2.4). The driver reads WD B's indicator from track 1: the bar lies straight, the switch is for them. The locomotive draws forward at shunting speed under the subsidiary, through WD B onto the north stub, and stops clear of the switch and short of the Limit of Shunt board at km 3.280 (Rule S 12): the stub is 60 m long and the locomotive 16 m, which is room enough and no more. The junction WD J lies 80 m further on and the run-round never reaches it. The subsidiary goes dark behind it.
  4. Round the loop. The driver changes ends. The box sets North stub → 2 → South stub: WD B reverse, WD A reverse, both locked; WD 6 changes to SHUNT and WD 4's subsidiary lights. Before setting back, the driver reads WD B's indicator from the stub: the bar leans towards track 2, which is where the move is going (Rule S 22). The locomotive runs Up through WD B onto track 2, along platform 2 past the standing coach on the other track, passes WD 4 under its subsidiary, reads WD A's indicator leaning towards track 2 as it trails through onto the south stub, and stops short of the Limit of Shunt board at km 2.900. It is now clear of WD A. WD 6 returns to SHUNT STOP and WD 4's subsidiary goes dark.
  5. Onto the train. The driver changes ends again. The box sets South stub → 1 for coupling: WD A normal, locked; WD 5 changes to SHUNT although track 1 is occupied. The driver reads WD A's indicator from the toe: the bar lies straight, so the switch will take the locomotive onto track 1 and not back onto track 2. The locomotive runs Down through WD A onto track 1 and approaches the coach at 5 km/h over the last 20 m, touching at 2 km/h or less. The coupler engages. The driver walks to the coupling, connects the brake pipe, returns to the cab and proves the brake through the train (Rule D 14). WD 5 returns to SHUNT STOP.
  6. Departure. Two minutes before time, Wending Box asks Ashgrove Box for the line. Ashgrove finds section A clear and no warrant out, issues warrant A to the train, sets Main → 1 so that AG 2 shows CAUTION, and answers. Wending sets 1 → S and WD 2 shows CLEAR. At the booked time, doors closed, J. Pell shows the green baton from platform 1. The driver sounds one short blast and takes the train away, Up, towards Ashgrove, holding warrant A until Ashgrove cancels it on arrival.

At Ashgrove the same thing happens with a headshunt instead of a stub: arrival under AG 2D and AG 2 at CAUTION to the ASHGROVE board, AG 4 into the headshunt, AG 5 and AG 3 round the loop to the stub short of the Limit of Shunt at km 0.400, AG 6 onto the train, AG 1 and T. Marrow's baton to leave. At Coldwater, CW 3, CW 6 and CW 4, CW 5, and CW 2 with A. Ashby's baton, do the same jobs in the same order.

Six signals and two switches per station is more than a small terminus strictly needs; a single ground signal and a promise would do. But every one of these signals stands where a driver has to make a decision, and a signal at that spot means the decision is never made alone. We would rather buy six lamps than one misunderstanding.

7.4 Block working and the Line Warrant

Between one station's home signal and the next lies a block section: a length of single line on which two trains cannot be allowed at once, because there is nowhere for one to stand aside. The Railway has three, and they are named by letter: section A from Ashgrove to Wending, km 0.420 to 2.880, worked by Ashgrove Box and Wending Box; section B from Wending to Coldwater, km 3.440 to 5.980, worked by Wending Box and Coldwater Box; section C from Wending to Fernhollow, branch km F 0.080 to F 2.030, worked by Wending Box and Fernhollow Box. Sections B and C both begin at the junction, and the same starter at Wending, WD 7 or WD 9, leads into either; it is cleared with the warrant for the section the switch is set for. Each section has exactly one Line Warrant, a numbered card kept in a slot in the box's register, and a train enters the section only when the warrant has been issued to it.

The exchange runs like this, and it runs the same way for every train in every direction.

  1. The request. Two minutes before a train's booked departure, the box at the departure end asks the box at the far end, by telephone, for line clear for that train: "Line clear for 1002 to Wending?"
  2. The issue. The far box looks at its track sections for the whole block and at its register. If the section is clear and no warrant for it is out, it enters the train against the warrant, sets its home route for the train (§ 7.2), so that its home signal shows CAUTION into the platform the train is booked for, and answers: "Line clear for 1002 to Wending. Warrant A issued; WD 1 cleared into track 1." If the section is not clear, or the warrant is already out to another train, it answers that the train must wait, and says why.
  3. The starter. Only now does the departure box set its starting route and clear the starter to CLEAR. The aspect is honest: the next signal, the far home, is showing a proceed aspect, and the section between is the train's alone. The box tells the driver: "Warrant A in hand. AG 1 cleared for the 08:00 departure."
  4. The run. The train departs on the starter and the baton (Rule S 18). While it is in the section it holds the warrant, and no other starter into that section can be cleared. The distant at the far end shows the driver, one warning distance out, what the far home is showing.
  5. Train arrived complete. When the whole train has come to a stand within the far station's limits, the far box cancels the warrant in its register and tells the driver: "Train 1002 arrived complete at Wending; warrant A cancelled." The section is free for the next request, in either direction.
Rule S 28Block working

A train enters a block section only with the section's Line Warrant. The box at the far end issues the warrant when the section is clear and no other warrant for it is out; the box at the near end clears its starter only with the warrant in hand; the far box cancels the warrant when the train has arrived complete. One warrant, one train, one section.

What the driver sees of it. The warrant is the box's business; the driver never handles it. What the driver sees is that a starter towards a section clears when it clears and not before, that the box says "warrant in hand" when it does, and that a starter at STOP two minutes before time means the far box has not yet given line clear, most likely because the other train has not yet arrived there. The driver waits. A starter is never passed at STOP because the timetable says the line ought to be clear; the warrant says whether it is, and the starter repeats the warrant.

A token that must be carried in the cab is the classical way of working a single line, and it is a good way. We chose a warrant that stays in the register and is issued over the telephone because our trains are electric and our line is short: the time a driver would spend collecting and surrendering a token at each end is most of the dwell time we have, and the failure we most feared was a token left on a windowsill. The warrant cannot be left anywhere. It can be issued to only one train, because there is one slot; it can be cancelled only by the box that can see the train standing in its station; and the starter cannot be cleared without it, because the same hand does both. When the instruments come, they will do exactly this, faster.

7.5 A worked example: the crossing at Wending

Trains 3 and 4 of the working timetable (Book T) are both motor cars. Train 3 is car 1002, leaving Ashgrove for Coldwater at 08:00; train 4 is car 1003, leaving Coldwater for Ashgrove at the same minute. They meet at Wending at 08:12, and this is what the three boxes and the two drivers do.

  1. 07:58, two requests. Ashgrove Box asks Wending for line clear for 1002 into section A; Coldwater Box asks Wending for line clear for 1003 into section B. Wending finds both sections clear and both warrants in their slots, issues warrant A to 1002 and warrant B to 1003, and sets two routes: Home S → 1 so that WD 1 shows CAUTION into platform 1, and Home N → 2 so that WD 8 shows CAUTION into platform 2. The two routes do not touch, and the frame allows both at once. Ashgrove clears AG 1; Coldwater clears CW 2.
  2. 08:00, two batons. T. Marrow shows the green baton to 1002 at Ashgrove; A. Ashby shows it to 1003 at Coldwater. Each driver sounds one short blast and leaves. Two trains are now on the line, one in each section, each holding its own warrant.
  3. The approaches. 1002 climbs the valley, reads WD 1D at CAUTION at km 2.680, brakes at the advance board, and sees WD 1 at CAUTION: in it comes to platform 1, stopping at the Down board at 3.165. 1003 comes down the 8‰ from Coldwater, shuts off power at the section board at 4.300 and takes it again at the resume board at 4.100, sounds one long blast at the whistle board at 4.900 for Millers' Crossing, reads WD 8D at CAUTION at 3.640 and WD 8 at CAUTION, and comes in to platform 2, stopping at the Up board at 3.015. Wending cancels warrant A as 1002 comes to a stand, and warrant B as 1003 does; both sections are free.
  4. 08:10, two more requests. Wending asks Coldwater for line clear for 1002 into section B, and Ashgrove for line clear for 1003 into section A. Both are given: each section was cleared by the arrival of the other train. Coldwater sets CW 1 into its platform, Ashgrove sets AG 2 into its; Wending clears WD 7 for 1002 and WD 4 for 1003.
  5. 08:12, two batons again. J. Pell shows the baton to each train in turn from its own platform. 1002 leaves Down towards Coldwater under WD 7; 1003 leaves Up towards Ashgrove under WD 4. Each carries a warrant for a section that the other has just left, and neither will meet anything until it meets a platform.

At 08:53 they do it all again the other way round, 1003 into platform 1 and 1002 into platform 2. The whole afternoon, and the whole of Book T, is this figure repeated.

Notice what the two platforms buy. With one platform, the second train to arrive would have to wait outside at the home signal until the first had left, and every crossing would cost a train the length of a dwell. With a platform on each track the two trains stand side by side, and the crossing costs nobody anything but the meeting itself.

7.6 Route locking

A route is more than a signal cleared over switches set the right way. From the moment its signal is cleared the route holds its switches: they cannot be moved by any lever, and no other route that needs one of them in the other position can be set, until the movement has passed clear. The frame does this by itself; no station master remembers it. This is route locking, and it is the reason the junction at Wending can be worked from one box by one person without ever moving a switch under a train.

A route is released, and its switches freed, when all of these are true, and not before:

  1. Its signal has been replaced to STOP. A signal is replaced the moment the movement passes it; a cleared signal that no train has yet reached keeps its route held.
  2. The approach is empty. The stretch from the signal to the first switch has no vehicle on it. A train that has passed the signal and is running towards the switch still holds the route.
  3. The sections of the route are empty, except the one the movement was cleared into, where it now stands: a train standing in the platform it was admitted to does not hold the home route behind it, and a train that has left for the section ahead does not hold the starting route once it has cleared the station's last switch.
  4. No vehicle stands within 12 m of any switch on the route, on any of its three legs, and none stands across it.
Rule S 32Route locking

A route holds its switches from the clearing of its signal until the movement has passed clear of them; no switch is moved under a route. Another route may share a switch only in the same position.

Two routes that need the same switch in the same position may be set together; the routes into platforms 1 and 2 from the south, for instance, share WD A in opposite positions and so exclude each other, while Home S → 1 and 1 → North stub share nothing and may stand together. At the junction it is simpler to say what may not: 1 → N holds WD J normal, so 1 → B, which needs it reverse, waits until the Coldwater portion has passed the junction and cleared the section beyond by the length of the lock; then WD J is free, the box sets the branch route, and WD 7 clears a second time. That waiting is what the second portion's driver sees as a starter still at STOP a minute after its time, and it is the frame doing its work.

An interlocking that only prevents a signal being cleared over a wrongly set switch protects the moment of clearing and nothing after it. The danger at a junction is the minute after: a switch moved for the next movement while the last is still on it. Locking the route until the train has passed clear closes that minute. We chose the 12 m and the empty approach over a track circuit for every metre because they can be checked by eye from the box as well as by the frame, and the eye is the frame's inspector.

7.7 A worked example: the split and the join at Wending

Train 7 of the working timetable (Book T) is two Class 1 motor cars in multiple, 1002 at the Down end driven from its cab and 1003 behind it. It leaves Ashgrove at 10:00 and is split at Wending: 1002 continues to Coldwater as train 7, 1003 follows over the branch to Fernhollow as train 31. In the afternoon the two portions come back and are joined at Wending into train 10 for Ashgrove. The driving is in Book D; the order in which the portions leave is the timetable's, in Book T. Here is what the signals do.

  1. Arrival, 10:07. Under WD 1D at CAUTION and WD 1 at CAUTION, train 7 runs into platform 1 and stops with the front of 1002 at the Down board at 3.165. Both cars stand on track 1. Wending Box cancels warrant A.
  2. The split. The driver secures 1002, walks to the coupling between the cars, parts the brake pipe and the coupler (Rule D 12), and returns to the cab. 1003 now stands behind, braked, with its own driver aboard. Nothing has been cleared yet; the box waits until it is told the train is in two.
  3. First portion, 10:12. Wending asks Coldwater for line clear for 1002 into section B; Coldwater issues warrant B and sets CW 1. Wending sets 1 → N: WD B normal and WD J normal, both now held, and WD 7 clears. The box says so, and says that WD J lies straight. At 10:14 J. Pell shows the baton to 1002; it leaves, reads WD J's indicator lying along the track as it approaches, and runs over the junction onto the main line.
  4. The lock does its work. At 10:14 Wending also asks Fernhollow for line clear for 1003 into section C; Fernhollow issues warrant C and sets FH 1 into its platform. But 1 → B needs WD J reverse, and WD J is held normal by the route 1002 is still passing over. The box tells 1003: "Warrant C in hand; WD 7 will clear once the route ahead is free." Once 1002 has passed the junction and drawn clear of it, 1 → N releases; the box sets 1 → B, WD J goes reverse and is held again, and WD 7 clears a second time, this time for the branch. The box says that WD J lies for the branch. At 10:16 the baton is shown to 1003, its driver reads the indicator at WD J leaning towards the branch, and it climbs away to Fernhollow.
  5. The join, afternoon. 1002 comes back from Coldwater under WD 8 at CAUTION into platform 2 and stands at the Up board at 3.015; warrant B is cancelled. Fernhollow asks for line clear for 1003 into section C towards Wending. Wending issues warrant C and sets Home B → 2 (call-on): WD J reverse and WD B reverse, held; and WD 10, the main head staying at STOP, lights its subsidiary. The box tells 1003's driver: "WD 10 shows its subsidiary: call on to platform 2 and couple to 1002 standing there." 1003 comes down the branch under WD 10D at CAUTION, passes WD 10 under its subsidiary as a shunting movement at 15 km/h, runs over the junction and through WD B onto track 2, and approaches 1002 at 5 km/h over the last 20 m, touching at 2 km/h or less (Rule D 10). The coupler engages; the pipe is connected; the brake is proved through the pair (Rule D 14). Wending cancels warrant C: 1003 has arrived complete, as part of another train.
  6. Departure as one, 11:00. Wending asks Ashgrove for line clear for the pair into section A, receives warrant A, sets 2 → S and clears WD 4. The baton is shown to 1002's driver, who takes both cars home.

While all this happens on platform 1 and then platform 2, the other pair, 1004 and 1001, is doing the same thing the other way about on the other platform: joined at 10:11 by a call-on under WD 10 into platform 2, split again at 10:47 on platform 1. Two split trains pass at Wending because the two platforms, the junction's lock and the call-on together let each portion reach its own line without ever meeting another on the north stub.

Which portion leaves first. A pair that has turned round at a terminus comes back with its cars the other way about: the branch car may now be at the Down end. The portion at the Down end must leave first, whichever line it is for, because the other cannot pass it. The timetable knows this; the frame merely refuses to let it be forgotten.

§ 8 One Train Working

Before the loops and the signals were built, the Ashgrove–Wending line was worked by one motor car, back and forth, with nothing between the buffer stops but two stop boards, four speed boards and two advance speed boards. The Railway still works it that way when the loops are out of use. This is how it is safe.

Under One Train Working the whole line, buffer to buffer, is one block section, and one vehicle holds it: it is, if you like, a single Line Warrant that is never surrendered. There is no other train to collide with, so there is nothing for a signal to protect, and the line has none. The driver departs each station on their own authority at the booked time (Rule R 24), stops at the stop boards, and obeys the speed boards. The only thing that can go wrong is a second vehicle, and the rule is simply that there is none: no locomotive is allowed onto the line until the motor car is stabled and the working is formally changed. Since the Coldwater extension the shuttle is worked this way only between Ashgrove and Wending, with the Wending switches clipped for track 1 and the line beyond closed.

Everything a driver learns on the shuttle is still true when the signals arrive. The boards do not move, the speeds do not change, and the stop is still made at the board. What changes is that the driver stops deciding whether the line is theirs, and starts reading whether it is.

§ 9 Failures and doubt

Signals fail. Lamps go, wires part, a plate falls off in a gale. The Railway is designed so that every failure looks like STOP, and the driver's response to every failure is the same.

9.1 What counts as a failure

  • A main signal showing no lamp, or two lamps, or a lamp of a colour you cannot name with confidence.
  • A ground signal showing no lamps, or one lamp, or lamps in a position that is neither horizontal nor the diagonal.
  • A signal whose plate is missing or unreadable, so that you cannot say which signal you are looking at.
  • A signal that shows a proceed aspect for a route you can see is not set: a switch lying the wrong way, a vehicle where a CLEAR says there is none.
  • A board that is missing, turned, or contradicts the last board you passed.
  • A distant that shows CLEAR while the home it repeats shows anything but CLEAR. The home is right; the distant is reported.
  • A starter at CLEAR when the box has not said "warrant in hand", or a warrant the register cannot account for. The train waits until the two boxes agree.
  • A junction indicator that disagrees with the box: WD J showing the branch when the box has said Coldwater, or the reverse. Stop short of the junction; the indicator is what the switch is doing, and the switch is what the train will do.
  • A home signal's subsidiary lit for a train that has not been told it is called on. Treat it as STOP and speak to the box.

9.2 What to do

  1. Stop. At the signal if you can; at once if you are already past it. Nobody is ever wrong to stop.
  2. Speak to the box. Use the telephone at the foot of the signal, or the cab radio. Say who you are, where you are, and what you see. "Service 201 at WD 1, signal dark."
  3. Do what the box tells you, and nothing more. The station master may authorise you past a failed signal at STOP, in words, for that one movement. Proceed at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of anything, as far as the next signal and no further.
  4. Write it down. Every failure, and every movement past a signal at STOP under authority, goes in the Incident Book with the time and the words used.

Never pass a signal at STOP on your own judgment because it "must have failed". A signal that has failed and a signal that is holding you for a train coming the other way look exactly the same. Rule S 10 has no exception for confidence.

9.3 When the signal and the baton disagree

A starting signal at STOP with a green baton, or a starting signal at CLEAR with a red baton, is a disagreement, and Rule S 18 settles it: whichever says stop is right. Stay, and speak to the station master. Most often the box has simply not caught up with the platform, or the platform with the box, and a moment's patience mends it. If the signal is at STOP and the station master insists, the station master may authorise the movement in words under § 9.2, and it is written down.

Two instruments that must agree before a train moves cannot fail towards "go" one at a time. That is the whole of the reason, and it is enough.

§ 10 Glossary

Words used in this book with a particular meaning. Book R holds the Railway's general vocabulary.

Advance speed board
A black numeral on an ivory triangle, announcing a lower limit one warning distance ahead. Be at that speed when the front of the train reaches the speed board it announces.
Aspect
What a main signal shows: STOP, CAUTION or CLEAR. Ground signals show indications: SHUNT STOP or SHUNT.
Baton
The station master's hand-held disc, green one side and red the other, with a lamp. Green held steady means Ready to start.
Block section
The single line between one station's home signal and the next: section A, Ashgrove to Wending; section B, Wending to Coldwater; section C, Wending to Fernhollow. One train at a time, under the section's Line Warrant.
Box
The signal box: the room, the frame, the register and the person working it. Ashgrove Box, Wending Box, Coldwater Box, Fernhollow Box.
Branch
The Fernhollow branch: the line that leaves the main line at WD J and climbs the side valley to Fernhollow, with its own kilometrage lettered F, a line speed of 40 km/h and its own block section, C.
Call-on
A home signal's subsidiary lit for a train: the platform ahead is occupied; enter at shunting speed prepared to stop short of the vehicles, and couple only as instructed. Only WD 8 and WD 10. Rule S 30.
Crossing
A place where a road or track crosses the line on the level: Millers' Crossing, km 4.700. Announced to the driver by a whistle board 200 m before it in each direction.
Distant signal
A two-lamp signal, green over amber, on an ivory chevron plate, one warning distance before a home signal, repeating what the home shows. CAUTION: be ready to stop at the home. CLEAR: the station is clear. Never at STOP. Named for its home with a D.
Cleared
Of a signal: changed from STOP to a proceed aspect, or from SHUNT STOP to SHUNT. Of a route: set and its signal cleared.
Facing move
A movement over a switch from its toe, which the switch may send one way or the other. The indicator shows which.
Down, Up
Down is towards increasing kilometrage (Ashgrove through Wending to Coldwater; on the branch, away from Wending); Up is towards decreasing. Odd signals face Down, even face Up.
Frame
The row of interlocked levers in the box that set switches and clear signals.
Gradient post
A slate post with a small ivory disc and two black arms at every change of grade, each arm tilted with the grade on its side and lettered in per mille (L for level). The arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. Information only.
Ground signal
A low black signal with two white lamps, governing shunting movements only.
Headshunt
The dead-end track beyond the inner switch of a terminus, used to run round. Ends at the buffer stop. Wending has none; its north stub serves.
Hectometre post
An ivory plate on a slate post every 100 m, reading the distance from the Ashgrove buffer stop as kilometre, point, hectometre digit (1.4). On the branch the plates count from the junction and carry the letter F (F 0.8). A position reference, not a signal.
Home signal
The main signal governing entry to a station from the single line: AG 2; WD 1, WD 8 and WD 10; CW 1; FH 1.
Junction
A switch that chooses between lines: WD J, at km 3.360, normal for Coldwater and reverse for the Fernhollow branch. Every movement over it is one route, which holds it until the movement has passed clear.
Kilometre post
The larger, green-rimmed plate at each full kilometre, reading the kilometre with its zero in the same black figures as the hectometre posts (2.0). A position reference, not a signal.
Limit of Shunt
A board on the single line beyond the outer switch, which shunting movements must not pass.
Line Warrant
The one numbered card for a block section, kept in the register of the box at the far end and issued over the telephone to one train at a time. No starter into the section clears without it; it is cancelled when the train has arrived complete. Rule S 28.
Main signal
A three-lamp colour-light signal on a post, governing every movement that reaches it.
Neutral section
The short gap in the overhead line where the Ashgrove and Coldwater feeds meet, km 4.190 to 4.210. No power is taken through it; section and resume boards mark it.
Normal, reverse
The two positions of a switch: normal for track 1, reverse for track 2.
One Train Working
Working a line with a single vehicle and no signals, the driver departing on their own authority. Now used only between Ashgrove and Wending.
Resume board
A black square with a white bar along the line, 90 m beyond a neutral section: power may be taken again.
Per mille
The Railway's unit of gradient, ‰: metres of rise or fall in each kilometre. The valley grade is 12‰, that is 12 m in every kilometre.
Portion
One of the parts of a train that is split at a junction station, each going its own way; and one of the parts that are joined there into a train.
Proceed aspect
CAUTION or CLEAR. Either gives the road as far as the next signal.
Route
A path through a station with its switch positions, governed by one signal (or a pair of ground signals).
Route locking
The holding of a route's switches from the clearing of its signal until the movement has passed clear of them. No switch is moved under a route; another route may share a switch only in the same position. Rule S 32.
Run through
To pass over a switch from a branch when the switch is set for the other branch, forcing the blades open. Damages the switch, can derail the tail of the move, and is always recorded (Rule S 22).
Section board
A black square with a white bar across the line, 90 m before a neutral section: shut off power.
Shunting movement
A movement within station limits under ground signals or a subsidiary, at up to 15 km/h, prepared to stop short of anything. It obeys main signals too.
Signal passed at danger
Passing a main signal at STOP or a ground signal at SHUNT STOP without authority. Always recorded.
Starting signal
The main signal governing departure from a platform track onto the single line: AG 1; WD 2, WD 4, WD 7 and WD 9; CW 2; FH 2. Cleared only with the Line Warrant in hand. WD 7 and WD 9 read over the junction and serve both lines beyond it.
Station limits
Everything between the speed boards at a station: the stub, the loop, the headshunt. Speed 25 km/h, shunting 15 km/h.
Stop board
The ivory board with a black bar at the end of a platform, where the front of an arriving train stops.
Stub
The short length of single line between a station's outer switch and its Limit of Shunt board. Wending has one at each end, the south stub and the north stub.
Subsidiary
Two small white lamps beside the head of a main signal (WD 4, WD 7, WD 8, WD 10). Lit diagonally with the main at STOP they show SHUNT to a shunting movement; to a train the signal is still at STOP, except at WD 8 and WD 10, where the lit subsidiary is a call-on.
Switch indicator
A black box on a short post at the toe of every switch, with a white bar on each face that lies the way the switch lies: along the track for the straight route, leaning towards the diverging track for the diverging route. Read from every leg. Not a signal.
Through station
A station with the single line at both ends, which trains pass through: Wending, with a platform on each loop track.
Track section
A length of track, or a switch, that reports to the frame whether a vehicle stands on it.
Trailing move
A movement over a switch from one of its branches towards the toe. The switch must be set for that branch; its indicator shows whether it is.
Train movement
A movement made under main signals, from station to station.
Warning distance
The distance between an advance speed board and the speed board it announces, and between a distant signal and its home: 200 m where the approach is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more. On this line, 250 m on the Up approach to Ashgrove and on the Up approach to Wending from the branch, and 200 m at every other approach.
Whistle board
A black W on an ivory disc, 200 m before a crossing: sound one long blast.
