§ 1 Principles

This book describes how the Railway speaks to its drivers from the lineside: with lights, with boards and, on the platform, with a disc held in a hand. It was written from first principles rather than inherited, and it tries to say everything once.

A signal is a promise. The box promises that the road is yours; you promise to go no further than the promise.Notice pinned above the frame, Wending Box

1.1 Eight principles

Everything in this book follows from eight decisions taken when the Railway was laid out. Where a later chapter seems arbitrary, one of these will explain it.

  1. One meaning per aspect. An aspect means the same thing at every signal on the Railway. There are no local variations, no special cases marked by a plate, and no aspect whose meaning depends on the one before it.
  2. Everything fails to STOP. A lamp that goes out, a wire that breaks, a lever that jams: each of them produces a signal at STOP, or a signal that must be read as STOP. Nothing that can fail can fail towards "go".
  3. A signal faces the trains it governs. A signal speaks only to the trains coming towards its face, and to no others. A driver never has to work out whether a signal is meant for them; if they can read it, it is.
  4. Two kinds of movement, two kinds of signal. A train movement runs from station to station under main signals. A shunting movement shifts vehicles within a station under ground signals. The two kinds of signal look nothing alike, and neither speaks to the other's movements.
  5. Fixed things are boards; changing things are lights. A board tells you something that is always true at that place: stop here, slow to this, shunt no further. A light tells you something that is true now. You never have to wonder whether a board might have changed.
  6. Every signal has a name you can say on the telephone. A station code and a number. Odd numbers face Down, even numbers face Up. Nothing else on the Railway is named like a signal.
  7. The signal is the box's voice; the baton is the station master's hand. To go, a passenger train needs both, where both exist. To stop, it needs only one.
  8. The signal stands where you stop. The position of a signal is the stopping place for a train it holds. There is no allowance for overlap to remember and no distance to estimate; the front of the train stops at the post.

Older railways accumulated their signalling one accident at a time, and their rulebooks show it: exceptions to exceptions, aspects that mean one thing on a branch and another on the main line. We had the rare good fortune of starting with a blank sheet and a valley to fill. We chose to spend that fortune on simplicity, because a simple rule is one that is obeyed at four in the morning in the rain.

1.2 Who speaks, who listens

The box
Each station has a signal box, named for the station: Ashgrove Box and Wending Box. The box holds the frame that sets switches and clears signals. At Ashgrove and Wending the station master works the box in person.
The station master
At Ashgrove, T. Marrow; at Wending, J. Pell. The station master sets routes, holds the baton, and is the authority for every movement within station limits. Messages from the box are signed "Marrow, Ashgrove" or "Pell, Wending".
The driver
Obeys every signal and board that faces them, obeys the baton, and drives no faster than the boards allow. Where the driver is in doubt, the driver stops. Book D describes the driver's work in full.
The Signal Engineer
Owns this book, the signals, and the frame. Nobody else alters a signal or its meaning.

Directions are as defined in Book R: Down is the direction of increasing kilometrage, from Ashgrove towards Wending; Up is the direction of decreasing kilometrage, from Wending towards Ashgrove. Kilometrage counts from the buffer stop at Ashgrove, km 0.000, to the buffer stop at Wending, km 3.300.

§ 2 Main signals

Main signals govern train movements. They are colour lights on a slate post, with three lamps in a vertical row and a black plate carrying the signal's name. They show the same thing by day and by night.

2.1 The head

The three lamps are always in the same order: green at the top, amber in the middle, red at the bottom. Only one lamp is lit at a time. The head is mounted on a slate post at a height where the driver sees it over the whole approach, and the plate beneath the head gives the signal's name in white on black.

2.2 The aspects

AspectShowsMeaning
STOPred, bottom lampStop and stay. The front of the train stops at the post.
CAUTIONamber, middle lampProceed; be prepared to stop at the next signal, stop board or end of track.
CLEARgreen, top lampProceed at line speed; the next signal shows a proceed aspect.
(dark, doubtful)nothing, or more than one lampTreat as STOP.
Rule S 10Signals at STOP

Never pass a main signal at STOP, and never pass a ground signal at SHUNT STOP. Bring the front of the train to a stand at the post and wait for the aspect to change or for the box to speak. Passing a signal at STOP is a signal passed at danger and is written in the Incident Book whatever the outcome.

Rule S 16Dark or doubtful signals

A main signal that shows no light, or more than one light, or a light you cannot read with confidence, is at STOP. Stop at it and speak to the box (§ 9).

CAUTION and CLEAR are both proceed aspects: they give you the road as far as the next signal. They differ only in what they tell you about what lies beyond. CLEAR promises that the next signal is also showing a proceed aspect, so line speed is safe. CAUTION promises nothing beyond the next stopping place, so you approach it able to stop.

The terminal-platform convention. A home signal that admits a train to a platform track ending at a buffer stop shows CAUTION when cleared, never CLEAR. There is no next signal, only the stop board and the end of the track, and the aspect says so honestly. At Ashgrove and Wending the home signals AG 2 and WD 1 therefore never show green.

Three aspects are enough for the Railway we have, and we resisted adding a fourth. Every extra aspect is one more thing to distinguish through a wet windscreen, and one more way for a tired driver to be nearly right. When the line grows and trains follow one another closely, we would rather add distant signals that repeat a home signal's aspect from further back than invent a new colour.

2.3 Kinds of main signal

Main signals are all the same to look at; they are named for what they do.

Home signal
Stands on the single line at the approach to a station and governs entry. Ashgrove's home is AG 2; Wending's is WD 1. A train held at a home signal stands outside station limits.
Starting signal
Stands at the end of a platform track and governs departure onto the single line. Ashgrove's starter is AG 1; Wending's is WD 2. A starting signal is cleared only when the section to the next station has been offered and accepted (§ 7.2).

Every main signal carries a telephone to its box at the foot of the post. If you are stopped at a signal and the box has not spoken to you, use it.

§ 3 Ground signals

Ground signals govern shunting movements: the short, slow moves within a station by which a locomotive runs round its train or vehicles are put where they are wanted. They are low, black and unmistakable.

A ground signal is a small black box on a short post, close to the ground, with two white lamps. It is placed at the point where a shunting move begins or must stop, and carries a name plate like a main signal. It shows one of two things.

IndicationShowsMeaning
SHUNT STOPtwo white lamps, horizontalDo not pass. Stand at the post.
SHUNTtwo white lamps, diagonal rising to the rightProceed as a shunting movement, at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of any vehicle or obstruction, as far as the route goes.
(dark)nothingTreat as SHUNT STOP.

3.1 What a SHUNT indication promises

A SHUNT indication promises less than a main signal does. It tells you that the switches on your route are set and locked, and that the box wants you to move. It does not tell you that the track ahead is empty. Very often it is not: the last move of a run-round is made under a ground signal into a platform track where a coach is standing, so that the locomotive can couple to it. That is why the indication carries its own speed and its own posture.

  • Shunting speed is 15 km/h (Rule R 10). It is a maximum, not a target.
  • Prepared to stop short means driving at a speed from which you can stop within the distance you can see to be clear. Approaching a vehicle to couple, that means 5 km/h over the last 20 m and contact at 2 km/h or less (Rule D 10).
  • As far as the route goes means to the next signal in your direction, to a Limit of Shunt board, or to the end of the track, whichever comes first.

3.2 Ground signals speak only to shunting movements

A ground signal is addressed to shunting movements and to nothing else. A train movement that has been given a proceed aspect at a main signal passes any ground signal within its route without regard to it; the main signal has already spoken for the whole route, and the ground signals along it stay at SHUNT STOP because nobody is shunting. So a Down train entering Wending under WD 1 at CAUTION passes WD 5 at SHUNT STOP on the stub without stopping, and has done nothing wrong.

This is not an exception to Rule S 10. The ground signal was never speaking to you. It is, though, the one place where a driver must know which kind of movement they are making, and the answer is always the same: if your last signal was a main signal, you are a train; if it was a ground signal, you are shunting.

We could have arranged for the box to clear every ground signal along a train's route as well as the main signal, and some railways do. It costs levers and it costs time, and it teaches drivers to expect the white lamps to move for them. We would rather they read the black box as what it is: a signal for a different job.

3.3 Switch indicators

Every switch on the Railway carries a switch indicator at its toe: a black box on a short post, standing on the side of the line away from the diverging track, with a white bar on each of its two faces. The bar lies the way the switch lies. Along the track, the switch is set for the straight route, which on this line is always track 1 (normal). Leaning towards the diverging track, it is set for the diverging route, track 2 (reverse). The box has a face towards the toe and a face towards the branches, so it can be read from every leg of the switch.

Read it from wherever you are coming from. From the toe (a facing move) the bar tells you which route the switch will take you onto: straight into track 1, or leaning off into track 2. From a branch (a trailing move, as when you set back from the headshunt onto track 2 during a run-round) the same bar tells you whether the switch is set for the track you are on or against you: if the bar leans towards your track, or lies straight and your track is the straight one, the switch is for you; otherwise it is set against you, and the points would be forced open by your wheels if you went on. The cab's list of what lies ahead names the next switch and reads its indicator for you in the same words: Switch WD B · set for you, or SET AGAINST YOU.

A route's signal promises that the switches are set and locked, and the frame makes that promise hard to break. But a promise is worth more when it can be checked, and the driver is the one person who is actually at the switch. The indicator lets them confirm, in one glance, that the points lie as the signal said; on a trailing move it is the last check before the wheels reach the blades. Running through a switch set against the move bends the blades and their rodding, puts the switch out of use until the Engineer has been, and can derail the tail of the very move that did it. We would rather spend a lamp on every switch than a morning on any of that.

Rule S 22Switch indicators

Before moving over a switch, read its indicator. Never run through a switch that shows set against your move: stop and speak to the Box. Running through switches is an incident.

A switch indicator is not a signal. It carries no authority to move and none to stop: the signal governs the move, and Rule S 14 still applies in full. The indicator tells you how the switch lies, which is a different question, and one you should always be able to answer before your wheels reach the points.

§ 4 Boards

Boards are fixed signs. They say something that is always true at the place where they stand. They are painted in the house colours on reflective stock so that they read by headlight at night as well as by day.

Rule S 12Limit of Shunt

A shunting movement stops short of a Limit of Shunt board facing it, and does not pass it under any indication of a ground signal. The board marks the edge of the station master's authority; beyond it is the single line, which belongs to the block.

4.1 Where boards stand

A stop board stands at the end of each platform in the direction of arrival, placed so that a train stopped at it has its doors along the platform. The Up stop board at Ashgrove is at km 0.135; the Down stop board at Wending is at km 3.165. A stop board faces the trains that stop at it and is invisible, in effect, to trains leaving.

A Limit of Shunt board stands on the single line a short way outside the outer switch of each station, facing the station: at Ashgrove at km 0.400 facing Down, at Wending at km 2.900 facing Up. The stretch between the outer switch and the board is called the stub, and it is exactly long enough for a locomotive to stand clear of the switch while it runs round.

Speed boards stand in pairs at the edge of station limits, back to back: a 25 facing the arriving train and a 50 facing the departing one. At Ashgrove the pair stands at km 0.450, at Wending at km 2.850. Each 25 is announced by an advance speed board one warning distance out on the single line, and the warning distance depends on the grade of the approach (§ 4.2): at km 0.700 facing Up for Ashgrove, 250 m out, because an Up train comes down the 12‰ valley grade to it; at km 2.650 facing Down for Wending, 200 m out, because a Down train climbs the last 6‰ to it. Between the pairs, on the single line, the speed is 50 km/h; inside them, 25 km/h; shunting, 15 km/h, whatever the boards say.

Gradient posts stand at every change of grade, on the post side of the line: at km 0.500, where the level of Ashgrove station limits gives way to the 12‰ climb up the valley; at km 2.400, where the climb eases to 6‰; and at km 2.800, where the line levels out for Wending. They are described in § 4.4, and the profile they mark is drawn in Book P.

A board is never lit and never changes. If a board seems to be wrong, missing or turned, do what the board would have said if it were there, report it to the box, and let the Signal Engineer sort it out.

4.2 Speed boards and advance speed boards

A speed board says what the speed is from where it stands. It is read against the whole train, not the cab: a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it. So a lower limit applies from the moment the front of the train reaches its board, and a higher limit applies only once the rear of the train has passed its board, because until then the tail is still on the slower stretch (Rule R 10). Leaving station limits, the driver waits for the last vehicle to clear the 50 board before notching up.

An advance speed board says that a lower limit lies ahead. It carries the numeral of the speed board it announces, on a triangle so that it cannot be mistaken for the board itself, and it stands one warning distance before that board. The warning distance is set by the grade of the approach: 200 m where the line up to the speed board is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more (Rule R 10). The driver must be at the announced speed when the front of the train reaches the speed board. Higher limits are not announced; there is nothing to prepare for.

We put the advance board 200 m out because a train at 50 km/h loses the 25 km/h down to 25 in about 145 m at a service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² on the level. On a 12‰ fall gravity takes back a quarter of that deceleration and the same braking needs about 190 m, so there the board stands 250 m out. What is left over in either case is the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the speed board itself is lost in fog or snow. On such a day the driver brakes at the advance board and does not wait to see the board it announces. Two distances, chosen by the grade, are still few enough for one habit: triangle, then brake.

Read with the posts: the Down advance board at 2.650 stands between posts 2.6 and 2.7 and its speed board at 2.850 between 2.8 and 2.9, with the last 6‰ of the climb between them; the Up advance board at 0.700 stands at post 0.7 and its speed board at 0.450 between 0.4 and 0.5, with the foot of the 12‰ fall and the gradient post at 0.5 between them.

4.3 Posts

Every hundred metres from the Ashgrove buffer stop to the end of the line at km 3.300 stands a hectometre post: an ivory plate on a slate post, carrying in black figures the distance from the origin in kilometres, a point, and the hectometre digit. The post at 1.4 km reads 1.4. At every full kilometre the plate is larger, bordered in green, and carries the kilometre alone in a green numeral under the small word KM: the post at 2 km reads 2. The posts stand on the left of the line in the Down direction; within the loops they are moved outward past track 2 so that they stay clear of every track.

A post is not a signal. It tells you where you are, never what to do; a train never stops or slows for a post. Its work is to give every place on the line a name that everyone shares. The Wending stop board stands between posts 3.1 and 3.2; the Ashgrove home signal AG 2 stands at 0.42, between posts 0.4 and 0.5; a rail that needs looking at is "at 1.7, on the Down side". The tables in § 6 give every signal's kilometre, and every one of them can be read against the posts from the cab.

To quote a position, give the last post you passed and, if you know them, the metres beyond it: "stopped at 2.7, about 40 metres on". A Box hearing "2.7" knows at once which side of the Wending speed boards you are, and what to send. Drivers also use the posts to judge braking: from 50 km/h the step-2 application for a stop board begins about a post and a half before it (Book D).

4.4 Gradient posts

The Wend valley climbs. From the Ashgrove buffer stop the line is level through station limits, rises at 12‰ from km 0.500 to 2.400, eases to 6‰ as far as 2.800, and is level again through Wending, which stands 25.2 m above Ashgrove. Down trains climb; Up trains descend. Every place where the grade changes is marked by a gradient post: a slate post carrying a small ivory disc at the top and two black arms, one reaching along the line each way. Each arm is tilted with the grade on its own side of the post and is lettered with that grade in per mille, or L where the line is level.

Read the post from wherever you are coming from: the arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. If that arm is tilted up and away from you, the line rises ahead; tilted down and away, it falls; level, it is level. The other arm tells you what you have just left. So the post at km 0.5 says to a Down train "level behind you, 12‰ up ahead", and to an Up train, reading the same arms from the other side, "12‰ down behind you, level ahead". The figures are the same from either side; only the sense of the tilt changes with the way you face, which is why the post needs no lettering for direction.

A gradient post is information, not instruction. Nothing about it tells a driver what speed to run or where to stop; it tells them what the line will do to the train, so that they can brake earlier on a fall, hold a train on a rise before releasing it, and know why the motors are working harder than the speedometer suggests. Book D gives the driving; Book P gives the profile the posts mark, drawn to scale.

Rule S 20Posts and gradient posts

Hectometre posts, kilometre posts and gradient posts are a position and profile reference and carry no instruction. Quote the posts when you report where a train, a fault or a person is; read the gradient posts to know what the line does ahead.

§ 5 Hand signals and the baton

The lineside speaks in lights and boards; the station master speaks with the baton. It is the oldest instrument on the Railway and the one we are fondest of.

5.1 The baton

The baton is a disc on a brass handle, green on one face and red on the other, with a small lamp at the centre that shows the colour of the face through a lens after dark. Each station master has one. It is shown from the platform, steadily, to the driver of a train standing at the platform.

Rule S 18Signal and baton

Where a starting signal and a station master both exist, a passenger train departs only when the starting signal shows a proceed aspect and the baton is shown green. Either at STOP holds the train. If they disagree, the one that says stop is right, and the driver stays.

The driver acknowledges the green baton with one short blast of the horn, which is also the blast that Rule R 18 requires before any movement from rest; one sound does both jobs. The full departure procedure, with the doors and the booked time, is Rule R 20.

A starting signal knows whether the line ahead is clear. It does not know whether a passenger is halfway through a door, or a parcel is still on the barrow, or the station master has something to tell the driver. The baton carries the human half of the decision. We could replace it with a lamp on a post and a button in the office, and one day, at a busier station, we may. It would work exactly as well and mean rather less.

5.2 Where there is no baton

At an unstaffed halt there is no baton. The driver departs on their own authority when the doors are closed and the booked time has come. On the shuttle line worked under One Train Working (§ 8) this is the rule at both ends.

5.3 Other hand signals

Any person on the Railway may stop a train by any means that reads as "stop": a red flag, a red lamp, both arms raised, a light waved from side to side. A driver who sees such a thing stops, and asks afterwards. Nobody is ever wrong to stop a train.

§ 6 Station layouts and numbering

Both stations on the line are laid out the same way, mirrored: a platform track and a loop, a switch at each end, a headshunt beyond the inner switch and a stub of the single line beyond the outer one. Here is every signal, board and switch, named and placed.

6.1 The numbering rule

Every signal is named by its station code and a number, written with a space: AG 2, WD 5. Station codes are two letters: Ashgrove AG, Wending WD.

  • Odd numbers face Down (they govern trains moving towards increasing kilometrage). Even numbers face Up.
  • Main signals take the lowest numbers, 1 and 2. Ground signals take the rest, 3 to 6.
  • Switches are named by station code and a letter: AG A is the outer switch on the single-line side, AG B the inner switch on the headshunt side. A switch is normal when set for track 1 and reverse when set for track 2. Every switch carries a switch indicator at its toe, which shows the way it lies (§ 3.3).

So a driver who hears "AG 3" on the telephone knows at once that it is at Ashgrove, that it faces Down, and that it is a ground signal, before they have looked at a diagram.

§ 7 Routes and the box

A signal is never cleared on its own. It is cleared as the last act of setting a route, after the switches have been set and locked and the track has been shown to be clear. This chapter is about that sequence, and about the frame that enforces it.

7.1 What a route is

A route is a path through the station from one signal to the place where the movement will stop, together with the switch positions that make that path continuous. Every route has a governing signal: the one main signal or the one or two ground signals that the driver reads to make the movement. A route is set when the switches are in position and locked; it is cleared when the governing signal has been changed from STOP to a proceed aspect or from SHUNT STOP to SHUNT.

The frame in the box holds a lever for each switch and each signal. The levers are interlocked: a signal lever cannot be pulled unless the switches on its route are set the right way, and a switch lever cannot be moved while a signal reading over it is cleared. The frame is also wired to the track. Each switch, and each stretch of track between switches and signals, is a track section which reports whether a vehicle stands on it, and the frame will not clear a signal over an occupied section.

Rule S 14Moving on a route

Move only when the governing signal has been cleared for the move you are about to make, and only as far as the route goes: to the next signal facing you, to a Limit of Shunt board, to a stop board, or to the end of the track. When the movement is complete, stop and wait; the route will be taken back behind you and the next one set.

The one deliberate exception. A shunt route whose purpose is coupling is cleared into an occupied track. The frame permits this only for the routes marked "for coupling" in the tables below, only for a ground signal, and only when the occupied section is the last one on the route. The driver approaches under Rule D 10.

The section between the stations. The single line from AG 1 to WD 1, and from WD 2 to AG 2, is one block section, and only one train may be in it. Before a starting signal is cleared, the box at the departure end asks the box at the arrival end whether the line is clear for the train; the arrival box sets its Main → 1 route, clears its home signal to CAUTION, and answers. Only then does the starter show CLEAR, which is honest, because the next signal is now showing a proceed aspect. The exchange is recorded in both boxes' registers, and one day it will be done by instruments; today it is done by telephone and a good deal of politeness.

7.3 A worked example: the run-round at Wending

Service 201 is a Class 4 locomotive with one Type C4 coach. It arrives at Wending from Ashgrove, locomotive leading, and must leave for Ashgrove with the locomotive leading again. Here is what the driver sees and does, signal by signal. The driver's side of it, cab by cab, is in Book D.

  1. Arrival. Having begun to brake at the advance speed board at km 2.650, the train passes the 25 speed board at km 2.850 already down to 25 km/h. WD 1 shows CAUTION: the platform is cleared for the train, and the track ends. The train passes WD 5 at SHUNT STOP without regard to it (§ 3.2), runs through WD A set normal onto track 1, and stops with its front level with the WENDING stop board at km 3.165. The locomotive now stands at the Down end of the coach, facing the headshunt.
  2. Station work. Doors open, passengers alight. The driver secures the locomotive, walks to the coupling, disconnects the brake pipe (both parts' brakes apply of their own accord) and parts the coupler (Rule D 12), then returns to the cab facing the headshunt.
  3. Into the headshunt. Wending Box sets 1 → Headshunt: WD B is already normal; WD 3 changes to SHUNT. The driver reads WD B's indicator from track 1: the bar lies straight, the switch is for them. The locomotive draws forward at shunting speed through WD B into the headshunt and stops clear of the switch, well short of the buffer stop. WD 3 returns to SHUNT STOP behind it.
  4. Round the loop. The driver changes ends. The box sets Headshunt → 2 → Stub: WD B reverse, WD A reverse, both locked; WD 6 and WD 4 change to SHUNT. Before setting back, the driver reads WD B's indicator from the headshunt: the bar leans towards track 2, which is where the move is going (Rule S 22). The locomotive runs Up through WD B onto track 2, along track 2 past the standing coach, passes WD 4 at SHUNT, reads WD A's indicator leaning towards track 2 as it trails through onto the stub, and stops short of the Limit of Shunt board at km 2.900 (Rule S 12). It is now clear of WD A. Both ground signals return to SHUNT STOP.
  5. Onto the train. The driver changes ends again. The box sets Stub → 1 for coupling: WD A normal, locked; WD 5 changes to SHUNT although track 1 is occupied. The driver reads WD A's indicator from the toe: the bar lies straight, so the switch will take the locomotive onto track 1 and not back onto track 2. The locomotive runs Down through WD A onto track 1 and approaches the coach at 5 km/h over the last 20 m, touching at 2 km/h or less. The coupler engages. The driver walks to the coupling, connects the brake pipe, returns to the cab and proves the brake through the train (Rule D 14). WD 5 returns to SHUNT STOP.
  6. Departure. Wending Box asks Ashgrove Box for the line. Ashgrove sets Main → 1, AG 2 shows CAUTION, and Ashgrove answers. Wending sets 1 → Main and WD 2 shows CLEAR. At the booked time, doors closed, J. Pell shows the green baton from the platform. The driver sounds one short blast and takes the train away, Up, towards Ashgrove.

At Ashgrove the same thing happens in mirror image: arrival under AG 2 at CAUTION to the ASHGROVE board, AG 4 into the headshunt, AG 5 and AG 3 round the loop to the stub short of the Limit of Shunt at km 0.400, AG 6 onto the train, AG 1 and T. Marrow's baton to leave.

Six signals and two switches per station is more than a small terminus strictly needs; a single ground signal and a promise would do. But every one of these signals stands where a driver has to make a decision, and a signal at that spot means the decision is never made alone. We would rather buy six lamps than one misunderstanding.

§ 8 One Train Working

Before the loops and the signals were built, the Ashgrove–Wending line was worked by one motor car, back and forth, with nothing between the buffer stops but two stop boards, four speed boards and two advance speed boards. The Railway still works it that way when the loops are out of use. This is how it is safe.

Under One Train Working the whole line, buffer to buffer, is one block section, and one vehicle holds it. There is no other train to collide with, so there is nothing for a signal to protect, and the line has none. The driver departs each station on their own authority at the booked time (Rule R 24), stops at the stop boards, and obeys the speed boards. The only thing that can go wrong is a second vehicle, and the rule is simply that there is none: no locomotive is allowed onto the line until the motor car is stabled and the working is formally changed.

Everything a driver learns on the shuttle is still true when the signals arrive. The boards do not move, the speeds do not change, and the stop is still made at the board. What changes is that the driver stops deciding whether the line is theirs, and starts reading whether it is.

§ 9 Failures and doubt

Signals fail. Lamps go, wires part, a plate falls off in a gale. The Railway is designed so that every failure looks like STOP, and the driver's response to every failure is the same.

9.1 What counts as a failure

  • A main signal showing no lamp, or two lamps, or a lamp of a colour you cannot name with confidence.
  • A ground signal showing no lamps, or one lamp, or lamps in a position that is neither horizontal nor the diagonal.
  • A signal whose plate is missing or unreadable, so that you cannot say which signal you are looking at.
  • A signal that shows a proceed aspect for a route you can see is not set: a switch lying the wrong way, a vehicle where a CLEAR says there is none.
  • A board that is missing, turned, or contradicts the last board you passed.

9.2 What to do

  1. Stop. At the signal if you can; at once if you are already past it. Nobody is ever wrong to stop.
  2. Speak to the box. Use the telephone at the foot of the signal, or the cab radio. Say who you are, where you are, and what you see. "Service 201 at WD 1, signal dark."
  3. Do what the box tells you, and nothing more. The station master may authorise you past a failed signal at STOP, in words, for that one movement. Proceed at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of anything, as far as the next signal and no further.
  4. Write it down. Every failure, and every movement past a signal at STOP under authority, goes in the Incident Book with the time and the words used.

Never pass a signal at STOP on your own judgment because it "must have failed". A signal that has failed and a signal that is holding you for a train coming the other way look exactly the same. Rule S 10 has no exception for confidence.

9.3 When the signal and the baton disagree

A starting signal at STOP with a green baton, or a starting signal at CLEAR with a red baton, is a disagreement, and Rule S 18 settles it: whichever says stop is right. Stay, and speak to the station master. Most often the box has simply not caught up with the platform, or the platform with the box, and a moment's patience mends it. If the signal is at STOP and the station master insists, the station master may authorise the movement in words under § 9.2, and it is written down.

Two instruments that must agree before a train moves cannot fail towards "go" one at a time. That is the whole of the reason, and it is enough.

§ 10 Glossary

Words used in this book with a particular meaning. Book R holds the Railway's general vocabulary.

Advance speed board
A black numeral on an ivory triangle, announcing a lower limit one warning distance ahead. Be at that speed when the front of the train reaches the speed board it announces.
Aspect
What a main signal shows: STOP, CAUTION or CLEAR. Ground signals show indications: SHUNT STOP or SHUNT.
Baton
The station master's hand-held disc, green one side and red the other, with a lamp. Green held steady means Ready to start.
Block section
The single line between one station's starting signal and the next station's home signal. One train at a time.
Box
The signal box: the room, the frame and the person working it. Ashgrove Box, Wending Box.
Cleared
Of a signal: changed from STOP to a proceed aspect, or from SHUNT STOP to SHUNT. Of a route: set and its signal cleared.
Facing move
A movement over a switch from its toe, which the switch may send one way or the other. The indicator shows which.
Down, Up
Down is towards increasing kilometrage (Ashgrove to Wending); Up is towards decreasing. Odd signals face Down, even face Up.
Frame
The row of interlocked levers in the box that set switches and clear signals.
Gradient post
A slate post with a small ivory disc and two black arms at every change of grade, each arm tilted with the grade on its side and lettered in per mille (L for level). The arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. Information only.
Ground signal
A low black signal with two white lamps, governing shunting movements only.
Headshunt
The dead-end track beyond the inner switch of a station, used to run round. Ends at the buffer stop.
Hectometre post
An ivory plate on a slate post every 100 m, reading the distance from the Ashgrove buffer stop as kilometre, point, hectometre digit (1.4). A position reference, not a signal.
Home signal
The main signal governing entry to a station from the single line: AG 2, WD 1.
Kilometre post
The larger, green-bordered post at each full kilometre, reading the kilometre alone under the word KM (2). A position reference, not a signal.
Limit of Shunt
A board on the single line beyond the outer switch, which shunting movements must not pass.
Main signal
A three-lamp colour-light signal on a post, governing train movements.
Normal, reverse
The two positions of a switch: normal for track 1, reverse for track 2.
One Train Working
Working a line with a single vehicle and no signals, the driver departing on their own authority.
Per mille
The Railway's unit of gradient, ‰: metres of rise or fall in each kilometre. The valley grade is 12‰, that is 12 m in every kilometre.
Proceed aspect
CAUTION or CLEAR. Either gives the road as far as the next signal.
Route
A path through a station with its switch positions, governed by one signal (or a pair of ground signals).
Run through
To pass over a switch from a branch when the switch is set for the other branch, forcing the blades open. Damages the switch, can derail the tail of the move, and is always recorded (Rule S 22).
Shunting movement
A movement within station limits under ground signals, at up to 15 km/h, prepared to stop short of anything.
Signal passed at danger
Passing a main signal at STOP or a ground signal at SHUNT STOP without authority. Always recorded.
Starting signal
The main signal governing departure from a platform track onto the single line: AG 1, WD 2.
Station limits
Everything between the speed boards at a station: the stub, the loop, the headshunt. Speed 25 km/h, shunting 15 km/h.
Stop board
The ivory board with a black bar at the end of a platform, where the front of an arriving train stops.
Stub
The short length of single line between a station's outer switch and its Limit of Shunt board.
Switch indicator
A black box on a short post at the toe of every switch, with a white bar on each face that lies the way the switch lies: along the track for the straight route, leaning towards the diverging track for the diverging route. Read from every leg. Not a signal.
Track section
A length of track, or a switch, that reports to the frame whether a vehicle stands on it.
Trailing move
A movement over a switch from one of its branches towards the toe. The switch must be set for that branch; its indicator shows whether it is.
Train movement
A movement made under main signals, from station to station.
Warning distance
The distance between an advance speed board and the speed board it announces: 200 m where the approach is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more. On the Ashgrove–Wending line, 250 m at Ashgrove and 200 m at Wending.
