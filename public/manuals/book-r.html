§ 1 Preamble

This Book holds the general rules of the Meridian Railway: the small number of agreements that let a driver, a station master and a train some kilometres apart act as one machine. Read it once through, then keep it in the cab.

A rule you cannot explain is a rule you should not have.General Manager's minute, first edition

A railway is a machine made partly of iron and partly of agreements. The iron is easy to see: rails, wire, wheels, the brass of a controller. The agreements are harder to see, and they matter more, because iron only does what it is told. When a train leaves Ashgrove, the driver has agreed to stop where the board says; the station master has agreed not to show the baton until the line is fit; and both have agreed, long before either was born, on what the word Up means. These agreements are the rules. Without them the iron is only scrap that moves.

Our rules are few. Where other railways have grown thick books by adding a rule for every mishap, we have tried to write down only what a careful person needs to know in order to act well, and beside every rule we have written why it exists. If a rule cannot be explained in a short paragraph, it goes back to the drawing office until it can. A rule that is understood is kept in the mind. A rule that is merely obeyed is kept in a drawer, and drawers are not open when it matters.

The rules themselves are written in the imperative, do this, because that is how a rule is used in the moment. The explanations are written by colleagues, in the plural, because that is what we are.

Where a rule is broken, or where something goes wrong without any rule being broken, the fact is written in the Incident Book. Chapter 10 describes the Book. It is a record and not a punishment. We keep it so that the rules can be improved and so that the next person on the same duty knows what the line has been doing lately.

We could have copied a great railway's rule book and struck out what did not apply. We chose instead to write from first principles, because a railway that understands its own rules can change them when the world changes, and a railway that has inherited them can only add to them.

1.1 How this Book is arranged

Chapter 2 fixes the words. Chapter 3 says who may decide what. Chapters 4 to 8 hold the rules, each with its number, its text and its reasons. Chapter 9 concerns the clock, Chapter 10 the Incident Book, and Chapter 11 puts every rule on one page for pinning in the cab. Signals have their own Book (Book S); the handling of vehicles has its own (Book D). Where this Book refers to them it cites the rule, for instance Rule S 10.

§ 2 Terms and directions

Words are the first piece of signalling equipment. This chapter fixes the ones we use, so that a message from a Box means the same thing at both ends of the line.

Kilometrage
Distance along the line, in kilometres, measured from the buffer stop at Ashgrove, which is km 0.000. The buffer stop at Wending is km 3.300. Every fixed object on the railway has a kilometrage, and every message that needs a place gives one.
Hectometre post
A slate post with an ivory plate, standing every 100 m along the line, that marks the kilometrage on the ground. The plate reads the kilometre, a point, and the hectometre digit in black figures: the post at 1.4 km reads 1.4. See § 2.1.
Kilometre post
The larger post at each full kilometre: an ivory plate with a green border and a green numeral under the small word KM, reading 2 for km 2.0. It takes the place of the hectometre post at that point.
Down
The direction of increasing kilometrage: from Ashgrove towards Wending. A Down train is one travelling Down. A Down signal governs Down trains.
Up
The direction of decreasing kilometrage: from Wending towards Ashgrove.
Station code
Two letters standing for a station on plates, in the timetable and in messages. Ashgrove is AG; Wending is WD.
Box
The signal box at a station and, by extension, the station master on duty in it. "Ashgrove Box" is the place; a message from it is signed "Marrow, Ashgrove".
Main line
The single line between the two stations, on which train movements run at line speed.
Station limits
The part of the line that belongs to a station: from the speed board on the approach, or from the home signal where there is one, to the buffer stop. Within station limits the speed is 25 km/h and the station master's authority applies.
Advance speed board
A black numeral on an ivory triangle, point up, standing one warning distance before a speed board that lowers the limit. It announces the lower limit; it does not impose it. The board it announces carries the same numeral (§ 4.1, Book S).
Warning distance
The distance from an advance speed board to the speed board it announces: 200 m on the Ashgrove–Wending line. It is enough to lose the difference in speed at a steady service deceleration with a margin for reaction, fog and snow.
Loop tracks
At a station with a loop, the two parallel tracks between the switches. Track 1 lies at the platform; track 2 alongside it.
Headshunt
The short dead-end track beyond the inner switch, used to draw a locomotive clear of the loop while running round. It ends at the buffer stop.
Stub
The short length of single line between the outer switch and the home signal, inside station limits. A locomotive running round reverses on it.
Train movement
The running of a train between stations, or from a platform onto the main line, under main signals and the timetable.
Shunting move
Any movement within station limits that is not a train movement: drawing forward, setting back, running round, coupling. Shunting moves are governed by ground signals and by the station master's instructions, and never exceed 15 km/h.
Secured
Of a vehicle: left in the state Rule R 22 describes, so that it cannot move on its own or at a touch.
Proved
Of a brake: tested from the cab and shown to act through the whole train (Rule D 14).
Baton
The station master's hand-held disc, green on one face and red on the other, with a lamp for night. See § 5.3.
Booked time
A time printed in the working timetable for a departure or an arrival.
Duty
A day's work for a driver and a vehicle, laid out in the working timetable under a number: Duty 101, Duty 201.

Forward and reverse belong to the cab and change when the driver changes ends. Up and Down belong to the line and never change. A message from a Box is written in Up and Down; a driver translates.

2.1 Posts and positions

Kilometrage is not only a figure in a table; it is written on the ground. From the Ashgrove buffer stop to the end of the line a post stands every 100 m, on the left of the line in the Down direction, and within the loops moved outward past track 2 so that it is never between the rails of anything. Each post carries the distance from km 0.000: the kilometre, a point, and the hectometre digit, so that the post at 1.4 km reads 1.4 and the next one Down from it reads 1.5. At every full kilometre a larger kilometre post stands instead, reading simply 2.

The posts are a reference and nothing more. They are not signals, they carry no instruction, and no rule turns on passing one. They tell you where you are, and they tell the Box where you are when you say so.

They also settle a question that is easy to lose on the ground: the numbers grow in the Down direction. If you are standing beside the line and cannot remember which way is Down, walk to the nearest post and read the next one.

Reporting a position. Give a position to the Box by the last post passed and, if you know it, the metres beyond it: "stopped at 2.7, about 40 metres on." A train's position is the position of its front. The Incident Book records positions the same way, so that a place in the Book can be walked to with nothing more than the posts.

A signal has a name and a platform has a stop board, but most of the line has neither. Without the posts, "a little past the speed board" is the best anyone could say, and the drawing office could not find the spot next week. With them, every hundred metres of the railway has a name that fits on a plate.

§ 3 Staff and authority

Two people work a train between Ashgrove and Wending: the driver, who is responsible for the train, and the station master, who is responsible for the station. Each has authority over their own part and none over the other's.

3.1 The driver

The driver is in charge of the vehicle, or vehicles, from taking over the duty until stabling: their preparation, brakes, lights, speed and stopping, the doors, and the safety of every movement. Only the driver decides to move, and nothing in this Book obliges a driver to move a train they believe unfit. A driver who is in doubt stops, and asks.

3.2 The station master

The station master is in charge of the station and everything within its limits: the switches, the signals, the platform, the passengers, and the order in which moves are made. The station master sets routes and clears signals, shows the baton, and instructs shunting moves. At the date of this edition the station masters are:

3.3 Instructions

Instructions from a Box are short, in plain words, and signed with the sender's name and station:

Draw forward to the headshunt. AG 4 cleared. — Marrow, Ashgrove

An instruction names the move, the limit of the move, and the signal that has been cleared for it. A driver acknowledges an instruction by carrying it out, or by a short reply if they cannot. Instructions that concern a train's departure are given by the baton (§ 5.3), not by message, so that the driver's eyes are on the platform and not on a slip of paper.

3.4 The limit of authority

An instruction can never authorise passing a signal at STOP. If a message and a signal disagree, the signal governs; the driver stops and asks. A station master who needs a train past a signal at STOP first clears the signal. If the signal has failed and cannot be cleared, the procedure in Book S applies and nothing in this Book shortens it. See Rule S 10.

We give each person one thing to be responsible for so that no one has to decide whose job it is. The driver never wonders whether the points are set; the station master never wonders whether the brakes will hold. Each trusts the other because each knows the other has nothing else to think about. And we put the signal above the instruction because a signal is a fact that both can see, while a message is a belief that one of them holds.

§ 4 Speeds

Speed is the one quantity that a driver alone controls and that everything else depends on. The limits below are upper bounds; a driver always runs at whatever lower speed the conditions ask for.

Rule R 10Speeds

Do not exceed the speed for the part of the line you are on, as follows.

Wherekm/hBegins
Main line50Once the rear of the train has passed the speed board 50 on leaving station limits, until the front reaches the next speed board 25.
Station limits25From the moment the front reaches the speed board 25 on the approach, or the home signal where there is one, and as far as the buffer stop. An advance speed board 25 stands one warning distance before it.
Shunting move15Throughout any shunting move, wherever it is.
Coupling approach5Over the last 20 m before a vehicle to be coupled.
Coupling contact2At the moment the couplers meet.

A limit applies to the whole train. A train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it: a reduction applies from the moment the front reaches its board, and a rise only once the rear has passed its board.

The station limit of 25 exists because a station is where the unexpected stands on the track: a coach left at the platform, a switch, a person with a barrow. At 25 km/h a driver can stop within the length of a platform on the service brake alone. Shunting is slower still because a shunting move is, by definition, heading towards something. And the coupling speeds are what the coupler and the passengers in the coach will forgive.

An overspeed is recorded in the Incident Book with the limit and the speed reached.

4.1 Where a limit begins and ends

A speed board marks a point on the line, but a train is not a point: it is a length, and every part of it is somewhere. So a limit is read against the whole train. Where the limit goes down, the front of the train is the part that reaches the board first, and the whole train must already be at the lower speed when it does. Where the limit goes up, the rear of the train is the last part still in the slow section, and the higher speed may be used only once the rear has passed the board that raises it. The short form is the one in Rule R 10: a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it.

A reduction is announced. One warning distance before every speed board that lowers the limit stands an advance speed board: the same numeral, but on an ivory triangle with its point up instead of a disc. It imposes nothing itself; it tells the driver that the disc with that numeral stands one warning distance ahead, and that the train must be at that speed when the front reaches it. On the Ashgrove–Wending line the warning distance is 200 m. At the service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² a train loses the 25 km/h between 50 and 25 in about 145 m; the remaining 55 m are the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the disc cannot be seen until it is close. The boards themselves are drawn in Book S; the way to brake between them is in Book D.

A rise is not announced. Nothing is lost by finding a higher limit a little late, so a speed board that raises the limit stands alone, and the driver reads it when the rear has cleared it.

The rear matters because the tail of a train does not know that the front has passed a board. A coach still in the station limits is still passing the switches, the platform end and whatever stands beside them, at whatever speed the driver has chosen for the front. And a reduction is announced because braking wants a distance, not a moment: with an advance board the driver can begin a steady, well-defined application and arrive at the lower speed exactly where it is required, in clear weather or in fog and snow alike, instead of guessing where the disc will appear and braking hard when it does.

§ 5 Station working

Most of what happens on a railway happens standing still. Stopping in the right place, opening the doors at the right moment and leaving at the right time are the rules the passengers notice, and so they are the ones we take most care over.

5.1 Stopping

Rule R 12Stop boards

Stop with the front of the train level with the stop board. A stop within 3 m short of the board is a correct stop. Passing the board is an overrun; stopping more than 3 m short is a short stop. Both are recorded.

The board is placed so that every door of a full-length train stands at the platform. At a terminus it is also what stands between the train and the buffer stop, and we would rather a driver aimed at a board than at the stop itself.

Approach a stop board as you would a signal at STOP: the brake goes on early, and the last metres are made at walking pace. A stop board faces the trains it governs; a board seen from behind is not yours.

5.2 Doors and dwell

Rule R 14Doors

Open the doors only when the train is stationary at a platform. Close them before moving. The traction circuit is inhibited while any door is open.

The time between arrival and departure is the dwell. The working timetable gives generous dwells, and the driver uses them: to change ends, to run round, to look along the train, to read the next line of the timetable. A dwell is not a delay.

The traction inhibit protects against the commonest mistake, but it does not protect a train that is rolling. Close the doors, then release the brake, in that order.

5.3 The baton

The station master's baton is a hand-held disc, green on one face and red on the other, with a lamp behind each face for night. It is shown from the platform, within the driver's view, and it is the only way a departure is authorised at a staffed station.

ShownMeans
Green face, held steadyReady to start. All conditions on the station's side are met. The driver may depart when the conditions on the train's side are also met.
Red faceStop. Do not move; if moving, stop.
Not shownNothing has been authorised. Wait.

The baton is shown until the train moves; if it is turned to red before the train moves, the authority is withdrawn and the driver stays. At an unstaffed halt there is no baton, and the driver acts on their own judgement.

5.4 Departure

Rule R 20Departure of a passenger train

Start a passenger train from a station only when all of the following hold at the same moment:

  1. The doors are closed.
  2. The booked departure time has been reached.
  3. Where there are signals, the starting signal shows a proceed aspect.
  4. Where a station master is on duty, the baton is shown green.

Then sound one short blast (Rule R 18), release the brake, and take power.

The four conditions may become true in any order, and it is usual for the baton to come last, because the station master waits for the doors. What matters is that all four hold at the moment the train moves. If any ceases to hold before then, the baton turned to red, the signal returned to STOP, the train stays.

Four conditions seem many for one departure. Each answers a different question. The doors ask whether the passengers are safe; the clock asks whether the passengers have had their chance; the signal asks whether the line is fit; the baton asks whether the platform is. No single person can answer all four, so no single person's word is enough.

At an unstaffed halt, conditions 3 and 4 do not apply: the driver satisfies themselves that the doors are closed, the time is right and the platform is clear, and departs. Under One Train Working (Rule R 24) both stations may be worked this way.

§ 6 Lights and horn

A train announces itself in two ways: by what it shows and by what it sounds. Both are messages to people who may not be looking at it, and so both are rules.

Rule R 16Lights

On the main line, the leading end of a train shows head (white lights) and the rear of the train shows tail (red lights). An end coupled to another vehicle shows nothing. Set the lights before leaving station limits.

On shunting moves within station limits, showing head in the direction of movement is recommended and not required.

End of the trainShows
Leading endHead: white.
Rear endTail: red.
Any end coupled to another vehicleNothing.

White ahead tells everyone which way the train is going. Red at the rear tells a person behind that the train is whole; a red light in the middle of a train is a coach that has been left on the line. When you change ends, the lights change ends too, and the walk between the cabs is where this is most often forgotten. Set the new rear before you leave it.

Rule R 18Horn

Before moving from rest, sound one short blast. When the baton has been shown, the blast is also the driver's acknowledgement of it.

A train that is about to move looks exactly like a train that is not. One blast gives the platform a second's warning and tells the station master that the baton has been seen. We ask for one blast, and a short one, so that it keeps its meaning.

§ 7 Securing a vehicle

A driver leaves the cab many times a day: to change ends, to couple, to look at something. Each time, the train is for a moment nobody's. This chapter is about that moment.

Rule R 22Securing

Before leaving the driving cab, in this order:

  1. Power controller to 0.
  2. Train brake to Full.
  3. Reverser to Neutral.

The pantograph may stay up. A cab left in any other state is unsecured, and the fact is recorded.

The rule applies to changing ends as much as to stabling. The two cabs of a car are two places; the walk between them is time in which the vehicle is unattended, and a vehicle standing at a platform is as capable of rolling as one standing anywhere else.

The brake is applied fully so that nothing rolls. The reverser is put to Neutral so that a controller knocked in passing, or found by a curious child, does nothing at all. Power is taken off first because a controller cannot be left at a notch while the reverser is moved, and because it is the right habit. Stabling proper, with the pantograph down and the lights off, is in Book D; this rule is the part of stabling that happens every time.

§ 8 One Train Working

The simplest way to keep two trains apart is to have one. This chapter describes the method used on the Ashgrove–Wending line before its signals were commissioned, and still used whenever the line is worked by a single vehicle.

Rule R 24One Train Working

On a line worked under One Train Working there is one train and no signals. The driver departs from each station on their own authority at the booked time, the other conditions of Rule R 20 being met. No second vehicle is placed on the line while the method is in force.

The safety of the method rests entirely on its name. A collision needs two trains; with one, the only things a train can meet are the buffer stops and its own stop boards, and the speed within station limits is chosen so that the driver can deal with both. There are no signals because there is nothing for a signal to say: the line ahead is always clear, and the driver knows it because they are the only one on it.

For the same reason the method is fragile in exactly one way. The moment a second vehicle is put on the line, even a trolley, even for a minute, the method is withdrawn and the line is worked under Book S. There is no half-measure between one train and two.

Duty 101, the Ashgrove–Wending shuttle worked by a Class 1 car, is under One Train Working. When the loops and signals at both stations are commissioned for Duty 201, the line reverts to signalled working, the station masters take up duty, and the baton returns to the platform.

We describe this method in the general rules, rather than leaving it to Book S, because it is the method against which every other method is measured. Anything more complicated than one train must earn its complication by being at least as safe.

§ 9 Timekeeping

The motto is not decoration. A railway is a promise about time made to people who are not on it yet.

9.1 The working timetable

The public timetable tells passengers when trains call. The working timetable tells the railway how: it adds the duty numbers, the vehicles, the dwells, the shunting moves and the minutes a passenger never sees. A driver works from the working timetable. The line for each station gives a booked arrival and a booked departure.

9.2 Early and late

A departure before the booked time is an incident and is recorded. A late departure or arrival is noted in the margin of the timetable and is not an incident. The reasons are different in kind. A passenger who arrives at the booked time and finds the platform empty has been failed by the railway, and no explanation will reach them. A passenger who waits a few minutes has been inconvenienced, and the cause is very often outside the driver's hands; the cure for lateness is in the timetable, not in the Incident Book.

9.3 Dwells

The dwells in the working timetable are generous on purpose. Changing ends means walking the length of a car; running round means five shunting moves and two walks; connecting a brake pipe means kneeling at a coupling. All of these take real minutes, and we would rather they were taken calmly. A driver who finds time in hand at a platform has not made a mistake.

We time the railway from the passenger's side of the doors. A minute spent properly securing a cab is invisible to them; a train that leaves without them is not.

9.4 The clock

The Box clock governs. The cab clock is set from it at the start of every duty. Where the two disagree during the day, the Box clock is right and the cab clock is set again at the next station.

§ 10 The Incident Book

Every railway keeps a record of what went wrong. Ours is kept in a way that we hope makes it easy to write in and useful to read.

10.1 What it is

The Incident Book is a ruled ledger, kept in each Box, with a copy made up weekly at the drawing office. An entry gives the time, the duty, the place by kilometrage (the last post passed, and the metres beyond it; see § 2.1), what happened in a line, and who wrote it down. The last column is headed What was learned. It is sometimes empty; it is the column the drawing office reads first.

10.3 What the Book is for

A clean duty is one with nothing written. That is the whole of the scoring on this railway: there are no points, and no one is ranked. The entries are read every week at the drawing office, in company, with the aim of improving the rules, the boards, the timetable or the vehicles. An entry that keeps appearing is a rule that is asking to be rewritten, or a board that is asking to be moved. Very rarely it is a person who is asking to be helped, and that is done quietly.

People do not write in a book that is used against them, and a book that is not written in is useless. We would rather know about every rough coupling than punish one. The last entry in the sample ledger above says nothing new; driver's second day, and that is exactly the kind of thing the Book is for.

The Incident Book is not a substitute for stopping. If a person is on the track, a vehicle is where it should not be, or a signal cannot be trusted, stop first and write afterwards. Nothing in this chapter delays the emergency brake.
