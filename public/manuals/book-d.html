§ 1 The driver

This Book is addressed to you. It tells you where you stand, what is in front of you, and what to do with it, in the order you will need it. Read it once from the beginning; afterwards you will only need the procedures and the handbooks.

A train does nothing by itself. Everything it does, somebody did first.Locomotive Superintendent's preface, first edition

1.1 Where you are

The Railway keeps account of where you are, because most things that matter can be done from one place only. You cannot raise the pantograph from the platform, and you cannot connect a brake pipe from the driving seat. At any moment you are in one of four places:

In Cab ASeated at the desk at the A end. The controls before you act on the whole train; forward means away from this cab.
In Cab BThe same desk at the other end. Everything is mirrored: forward now means the opposite direction along the track.
On the groundBeside your vehicle, on the platform or the ballast. From here you can walk to either cab or to a coupling.
At a couplingStanding between two vehicles, or at the free end of one. Here you connect and disconnect the brake pipe and part the coupler.

Walking takes time. A person walks at about 1.2 metres a second, so the length of a Class 1 car, twenty-two metres, is nearly twenty seconds of your day, and the steps up and down add a few more. A change of ends on a locomotive and coach is a longer walk still, and you will do it twice on Duty 201. Allow for it in the dwell.

To do thisYou must be
Drive, brake, raise or lower the pantograph, set the lights at this end, work the doors, sound the horn, run the brake testin the cab at that end
Set the lights at the other endin the cab at the other end
Walk to the other cab or to a couplingon the ground
Connect or disconnect a brake pipe, part a couplerat that coupling

We could have given every cab a switch for every lamp on the train, and a lever that couples at a distance. We did not, because a driver who has to walk the length of the train sees the train. A brake pipe that is connected by hand is a brake pipe that somebody has looked at.

1.2 Leaving a cab

Whenever you leave a driving cab, for any reason, secure the train first. The rule is in Book R and it is short enough to learn by heart.

Rule R 22Securing (cited)

Before leaving the cab: power controller to 0, train brake to Full, reverser to Neutral, and the parking brake applied where the vehicle stands on a gradient or is to be left unattended. The pantograph may stay up. See Book R, Rule R 22.

The cab will not let you out of the door while the train can move: the C key does nothing until the conditions are met, and the message panel tells you which one is missing. On a gradient that includes the parking brake; on the level at a platform it does not, unless you are stabling. Leaving a cab that is not secured is entered in the Incident Book, so treat it as a habit rather than a check.

You may leave the pantograph up when you go to the other cab. On a cold morning the heating is on the traction supply and the passengers will thank you for it.

§ 2 The cab

Every driving cab on the Railway has the same desk. The Class 1 car and the Class 4 locomotive differ in size, weight and strength, but the levers fall under the same hands in the same order, so that a driver who knows one cab knows them all.

2.1 Controls

Reverser R N F

Three positions: Reverse, Neutral, Forward. They are relative to the cab you are sitting in: Forward drives the train away from your cab, Reverse pulls it towards you. The reverser will not move unless the power controller is at 0. In Neutral the motors are disconnected and the controller does nothing.

A reverser that could be thrown under power would be a reverser that one day is thrown under power, with the motors still turning. We interlock it so that the question never arises.

Power controller W up · S down

Five notches, 0 to 4. Each notch asks the motors for a further quarter of the tractive effort available at the present speed. The controller has effect only when all of the following are true: the reverser is in Forward or Reverse, the pantograph is up and the line is live, and the doors are closed. Otherwise the notch is recorded and nothing happens; the train will start as soon as the missing condition is met, so do not leave a notch on the controller while you wait.

Train brake A release · D apply · Space emergency

Six positions: Release, 1, 2, 3, Full, Emergency. The D key moves the handle one step towards apply, the A key one step towards release. Space throws it straight to Emergency from anywhere.

The brake is an automatic air brake, and it repays a moment's understanding. A pipe runs the length of the train, joined at every coupling. When you place the handle in Release, the pipe is charged to 5.0 bar and every brake on the train releases. Each step towards apply drops the pipe pressure, and each vehicle answers the drop by applying its own brakes in proportion; at 3.5 bar, the Full position, every brake is fully applied. Emergency vents the pipe to nothing, as fast as air can leave it. The important consequence is this: if the pipe is parted anywhere, by uncoupling or by accident, the air escapes and the brakes apply themselves on both parts of the train. The brake fails safe. It cannot be forgotten, only released.

Releasing takes longer than applying. The pipe recharges at about half a bar a second, so a full release from Full takes three seconds or so, and from Emergency the better part of ten. Plan your starts accordingly.

Parking brake B

Two positions, Off and On. It is a mechanical brake on this vehicle only, needing no air, and it holds about half the full service force: enough to hold the car, or the locomotive with its coach, on any grade the Railway has. It is the brake that holds a stabled vehicle overnight and the brake you apply before leaving a cab on a gradient (Rule D 22). Release it before you move, as the first thing you do after entering a cab; a train that moves with it applied drags it, and the Incident Book says so.

The air brake is a fine brake and a poor anchor. Its air leaks away over hours, and it cannot be told the vehicle is to be left alone. A parking brake is a screw and a rod: it holds until somebody takes it off, which is exactly the property an anchor wants.

Pantograph P

Two positions, Down and Up. Raising takes a few seconds; the Line lamp lights when the pantograph is touching a live wire. Without it there is no traction, no heating and no lights. The whole line, headshunts included, is wired at 1.5 kV, so on the Ashgrove–Wending line you may raise it where you stand.

Lights, this end L cycles

Three positions: Off, Tail, Head. The switch in a cab controls the lamps at that end only. Head shows two white lamps, Tail two red ones. On the main line the leading end of a train shows Head, the rear of the train shows Tail, and a coupled end shows nothing (Rule R 16). The Type C4 coach looks after its own tail lamps; see § 10.

Doors O

Two positions, Closed and Open. Open them only when stopped at a platform, and close them before you move (Rule R 14). Traction is inhibited while any door is open; the Doors lamp shows amber until they are all closed and locked.

Horn H

The horn sounds for as long as the key is held, and a blast is judged by its length: released within a second and a half it is a short blast; held a second and a half or longer it is a long blast. A tap gives the short one and a deliberate hold gives the long one, and the Messages tab records which you gave, as One short blast. or One long blast. The Horn button on the panel works the same way, pressed and held with the mouse. Sound one short blast before every movement from rest (Rule R 18), including shunting moves and including the second half of a change of ends. Nobody on the platform has to guess. The long blast belongs to the whistle board (§ 4.9).

Brake test button

Runs the brake continuity test described in § 6. It takes about twenty seconds; do not touch the brake handle while the Test lamp is lit.

Leave cab / enter cab C

From a secured cab, puts you on the ground beside it. From the ground beside a cab, seats you at its desk. Walking between places is done from the ground with the walk buttons on the panel.

§ 3 Preparing a stabled vehicle

A stabled vehicle stands with its pantograph down, its lights off, its doors closed, its train brake applied and its parking brake on. You arrive on the ground beside it with fifteen minutes in hand. This is how those fifteen minutes go.

  1. Decide which end will lead. On the duty sheet the first departure has a direction; the cab facing that way is the leading cab, the other is the rear cab.
  2. Walk to the rear cab and enter it. Set the lights at this end to Tail. Leave the cab; it is already secured because nothing has been touched.
  3. Walk to the leading cab and enter it.
  4. Parking brake Off. It has held the vehicle all night; the train brake, in Full, holds it now. Make this the first thing you do in any cab you mean to drive from (Rule D 22).
  5. Pantograph Up. Wait for the Line lamp. Nothing else in the cab is alive until it lights.
  6. Lights at this end to Head.
  7. Brake handle to Release and watch the pipe rise to 5.0 bar, then back to Full. You have just proved that the brake on your own vehicle answers the handle.
  8. If you are coupled to anything, run the Brake test (§ 6.3). The lamp goes out when the pipe has been proved to the far end.
  9. Check the Doors lamp is out. If passengers are to board, open the doors now, on the platform side, and close them before departure.
  10. Reverser to Forward. The train is prepared. Sit with the brake in Full until the departure procedure in § 4.1 lets you go.

We put the walk to the rear cab first, before the pantograph goes up, so that you are never on the ground beside a live train that you have already made ready to move. It also means the rear lamps are lit before anybody has to remember them.

On a locomotive coupled to a coach, the rear of the train is the coach, and the coach lights its own tail lamps. The locomotive's coupled end stays at Off. Steps 2 and 6 then apply only to the locomotive's leading cab.

The parking brake is one brake on one vehicle; it does not care which cab you release it from. If you released it in the rear cab by habit, it stays released. Step 4 simply makes sure it is off before anything else can move the train.

§ 4 Running

Driving on the Meridian Railway is mostly waiting for the right moment and then doing a small thing gently. The rules below say when the moment has come. The handbooks in § 8 and § 9 say how gently.

4.1 Starting

A passenger train leaves a station when, and only when, all of the conditions in Rule R 20 are met. In the order you will check them:

  1. Doors closed. The Doors lamp is out.
  2. Booked time reached. The clock shows the departure time on the duty sheet, or later. Never earlier; an early departure is an Incident Book entry, and a passenger left on the platform.
  3. Starting signal cleared, where there are signals. The starter for your platform shows Clear or Caution. On a line worked by one train with no signals, this condition does not exist (Rule R 24) and you go on your own authority at the booked time.
  4. Baton shown, where a station master is on duty. The station master stands on the platform and shows you the green face of the baton, steadily. Until then you wait, whatever the signal says. Where there is no station master there is no baton.
  5. Horn. One short blast: a tap on H, not a hold.
  6. Brake to Release. Watch the pipe reach 5.0 bar.
  7. Controller to notch 1, and as the train begins to move, notch 2. Notch up further only once the platform is behind you.

Steps 3 and 4 are separate on purpose. The signal tells you the line is yours; the baton tells you the platform is done with you. A cleared signal and a station master still helping a passenger with a bicycle are not a departure.

4.2 Speeds

Rule R 10 gives four speeds, and you will use all four on most duties.

A limit applies to the whole train, and a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it (Rule R 10). A lower limit is yours from the moment the front reaches its board: a train may not pass a 25 board at 40 on the grounds that it is slowing down. A higher limit is yours only once the rear of the train has passed its board: leaving station limits, the whole train must be past the 50 board before you notch up. The Limit indicator keeps this account for you; it falls as the front reaches a lower board and rises only when the last vehicle has cleared a higher one.

The tail of the train is still in the slow section until it has passed the board. A locomotive that opens up the moment its cab passes the 50 board drags its coach through station limits at forty. Reductions are announced ahead, so that the front can be at the new speed when it arrives; rises are not, because nothing is lost by waiting a train-length.

Braking for a reduction

A reduction is announced one warning distance ahead by an advance speed board: the same black numeral, on an ivory triangle with its point up (Book S, § 4). The warning distance is 200 metres where the approach to the speed board is level or rising, and 250 metres where it falls at 10‰ or more (Rule R 10). On the Ashgrove–Wending line the advance board for the 25 at 2.85 stands at 2.65 running Down, fifty metres beyond post 2.6, on a rising approach; the one for the 25 at 0.45 stands at 0.70 running Up, at post 0.7, because an Up train comes down the 12‰ valley grade to within fifty metres of the board. Rises get no advance board: a 50 board simply stands where the 50 begins.

  1. At the advance board, step 2. On the Class 1 car, and on the Class 4 with one coach, step 2 gives about 0.5 m/s² on the level.
  2. Count the distance. At that rate a train loses the twenty-five between 50 and 25 in about 145 metres on the level, and about 190 metres on a 12‰ fall, where gravity takes back a quarter of the brake. Either way you reach the speed board with some fifty to sixty metres in hand. That margin is the allowance for reaction, for fog and for snow; it is not an invitation to apply later.
  3. Check at the speed board. Be at 25 as the front reaches it. If you are above, step 3 at once and note it; if you are well under, ease to step 1, but do not release before the board.
  4. Hold it. Release once at 25 and keep 25: the Limit indicator shows 25 through station limits and will not show 50 again until the rear of the train has cleared the 50 board on the way out.

In fog or falling snow, brake at the advance board without waiting to see the speed board. The board will arrive when the count says it will, and the posts confirm the count: running Down, 2.7 and 2.8 pass while you brake and the board is fifty metres beyond 2.8; running Up, you brake at 0.7, 0.6 and 0.5 pass, the grade levels out at 0.5, and the board is fifty metres beyond it.

4.3 Braking to a stop board

Every platform has a stop board: an ivory board with a thick black bar, the station's name beneath. Rule R 12 asks you to stop with the front of the train level with it; anywhere within three metres short of the board is a correct stop. Passing the board is an overrun and is recorded. Stopping more than three metres short is recorded too, though nobody will think worse of you for it.

A working rule of thumb for the Class 1 car on the level:

  1. At 50 km/h, place the handle in step 2 at about 150 metres from the board. The Ahead indicator gives the distance; so do the posts beside the line, below.
  2. Watch the speed fall. If the board is coming up faster than the speed is falling, go to step 3; if you are going to stop short, come back to step 1. Do not saw between Release and Full.
  3. In the last few metres, ease to step 1. The train settles to a stop without a jolt and the front comes to rest a metre or two short of the bar.
  4. Handle to Full before you touch the doors.

A locomotive with one coach is heavier but has more brake for its weight; you will find step 2 from about 120 metres is enough. Do not be proud about it: a smooth stop three metres short is worth more than a perfect one that spills the tea.

Judging distance by the posts

The Ahead indicator is a courtesy; the posts are the railway. A hectometre post stands every hundred metres along the line, on your left as you run Down, an ivory plate on a slate post reading the distance from the Ashgrove buffer stop: the post at 1.4 km reads 1.4. Every full kilometre has a larger kilometre post, the same plate on a larger scale with a green rim, reading the kilometre with its zero, as 2.0. Within the loops the posts stand outward of track 2. They carry no instruction; they tell you where you are, and from that, how far you have to go.

Coasting

Between stations there is no virtue in power for its own sake. Once at line speed, come back to notch 0 and let the train run; on the level it loses about a kilometre an hour every few seconds, which is slower than you would think. Power again only if the speed falls below what the timetable needs. The 25 board is a good place to be coasting already.

4.4 The emergency brake and the buffer stop

Rule D 18Emergency brake

When in doubt, use it. The Emergency position vents the pipe and applies every brake on the train as hard as it will go. Its use is recorded in the Incident Book as a note, not as a fault.

A driver who fears the emergency brake will hesitate. We write down every use so that we can learn why it was needed, and we say plainly that using it is never the wrong answer. The wrong answer is the collision.

Rule D 16Buffer stops

Do not strike them. Every headshunt and platform on the line ends at a buffer stop carrying a fixed red lamp. Treat the lamp as a signal at Stop that will never clear.

The buffer stop at the end of a headshunt is close behind the inner switch, eighty metres at Ashgrove and Wending. A locomotive drawing forward from the platform must be at shunting speed and braking as soon as its rear end is clear of the switch.

4.5 Gradients

The Wend valley climbs. Ashgrove lies at the foot of it, Wending stands 25 metres higher, by the weir, and Coldwater 43 metres up, at the quarry; the line rises between the stations and is level only within their limits. Down trains climb, Up trains descend, and the difference between the two is the subject of this section.

What a grade does to a train is simple arithmetic. On 12‰, gravity pulls the Class 1 car with 4.5 kN and the locomotive and coach with 11 kN, about a tenth of the tractive effort either has at line speed: both climb easily, in notch 2, and both roll away if left unbraked. A released car on 12‰ gathers a tenth of a metre a second every second, and is at walking pace within fifteen seconds. Nothing on this line is steep. Everything on it moves by itself.

Starting on a rise

Rule D 20Starting on a rise

Hold the train on the train brake, apply power, and release the brake as the traction takes up. Rolling back more than half a metre is an incident.

  1. Handle in Full, or step 3 at least: the train is held.
  2. Reverser Forward, controller to notch 1, then notch 2. The motors are pulling against the brake; nothing moves yet.
  3. Handle to Release. The pipe charges over three seconds and the brakes come off gradually; by the time they are off, the traction is more than the hill, and the train moves the way you mean it to.
  4. If the speed indicator shows movement before you have released, you have rolled: step 3 at once, and begin again with more notch.

Released first, a train on 12‰ starts to roll before the motors have anything to say; a driver who then notches up in a hurry starts with a jerk, or with wheelspin. Power first and brake after is the order that costs nothing and never surprises anybody, so it is the order for every start, level or not.

Descending

Coasting down 12‰ builds speed: about a third of a kilometre an hour every second on the car, rather more on the heavier train. From 50 km/h you would be over the limit in a quarter of a minute. Hold the speed with the brake, not with the controller: a step 1 application checks the car on the 12‰, a step 2 application slows it, and the right hand moves between Release and step 1 as the speed drifts. On the 6‰ stretch step 1 alone is nearly a balance. The Up run from Wending is the descent: level to the 25 board at 2.85, then 6‰ from post 2.8, 12‰ from 2.4, and level again from 0.5.

The reduction at Ashgrove, on the fall

An Up train reaches the Ashgrove reduction going downhill, which is why its advance board stands at 0.70 and not at 0.65 (§ 4.2). At 0.5 m/s² on a 12‰ fall you lose the twenty-five between 50 and 25 in about 190 metres, not 145, because gravity is working against the brake all the way. Make the step-2 application at the triangle at post 0.7, hold it, and be at 25 by the disc at 0.45; the grade levels out at 0.5, fifty metres before the board, which is where you find the last of your margin. In fog, the same, by the posts.

Securing on a gradient

Rule D 22Parking brake

Release the parking brake before moving, as the first thing after entering the cab. Apply it before leaving the cab on a gradient, and whenever the vehicle is stabled. Moving with it applied, or a vehicle moving with no direction set, is an incident.

Wherever the gradient indicator shows anything but level, Rule R 22 adds the parking brake to the three conditions for leaving a cab, and the door will not open without it. The stations are level, so a run-round never needs it; a change of ends after a stop on the valley grade always does. A vehicle that moves with no direction set, with the reverser in Neutral or nobody in a cab, is a runaway, and the Incident Book records it under that word. A train that moves with the parking brake still applied is dragging it; that is recorded too, after three metres.

The Type C4 coach has no parking brake of its own. Uncoupled, it is held by its automatic brake, which applies when the pipe is parted; that is safe on the level platforms where run-rounds happen, and it is the reason a coach is never left uncoupled on the grade.

4.6 Switches

A switch has three legs: the toe, where the single track begins to divide, and two branches beyond it, the straight route and the diverging one. Every move over a switch is one of two kinds. A facing move comes in at the toe and is sent one way or the other by the switch: which way is not your decision, and you had better know it. A trailing move comes in along a branch and out through the toe: if the switch lies for your branch you pass over it; if it lies for the other branch, your wheels force the blades across and you have run through it, which bends the blades, breaks the rodding, and can put the tail of your move on the ground.

Every switch on the Railway now carries a switch indicator at its toe: a black box on a short post with a white bar on each face. The bar lies the way the switch lies. Along the track, the switch is set for the straight route; leaning towards the diverging track, it is set for the diverging route. It reads the same from every leg. Coming at the toe, it tells you which route you are about to take. Coming along a branch, it tells you whether the switch is set for your track or against you, and the Ahead indicator (§ 2.2) says so in those words.

Rule S 22Switch indicators

Before moving over a switch, read its indicator. Never run through a switch that shows set against your move: stop and speak to the Box. Running through switches is an incident. (Book S; cited here.)

The indicator carries no authority. A ground signal showing Shunt is the station master's word that the route is set and the move may be made; the indicator is the switch's own word that it lies as the signal promised. The two agree almost always. When they do not, the indicator is the one telling the truth about the rails, and the rails are what you are about to run over: stop short of the switch and tell the Box what you see. Make it a habit, on every move that crosses a switch, to glance at the indicator as you approach it, the way you glance at a signal. It costs a second; the alternative costs a switch.

A driver in a cab cannot see the blades of a switch; they are low, small and on the far side of the wheel. A signal says what was intended for the route; only the indicator says what the switch has actually done. On a run-round you cross four switches in six moves, half of them trailing, and the station master is throwing the points between your moves. The indicator is how you check that the last throw happened before you trust your tail to it.

Rule S 24Distant signals

At a distant at Caution, reduce speed so as to be able to stop at the home signal it repeats. A distant at Clear promises a clear run through the station. (Book S; cited here.)

The method is the one you already have for the advance speed board, because the two stand together: the distant is placed at the same warning distance as the triangle, 200 m on a level or rising approach and 250 m on a fall of 10‰ or more, and it asks the same of you. On Duties 201 and 301 every home you meet shows Caution when it is cleared, because you stop at every station; the distant will therefore be at Caution on the approach, and that is right: it is telling you to expect to stop, and you are going to.

A home signal stands where the station begins, which is too late to learn that it is at Stop if you arrive at line speed. The distant moves the news back by a braking distance, so that a driver who cannot see the home, in fog or snow or round the shoulder of the valley, still knows what to do. It is not a signal to stop at; passing a distant at Caution is what you are meant to do. It is a signal to slow at, and to believe.

The Ahead indicator names a distant with its home, as WD 1D (distant for WD 1) · CAUTION, and shows the aspect of the home itself a little further down the list. In the cab there is no such courtesy: the distant is what you have until the home comes into view.

4.8 Neutral sections

Between Wending and Coldwater the overhead line is fed from both ends, from the Ashgrove substation and the Coldwater one, and the two feeds meet at a neutral section at post 4.2: twenty metres of wire, from 4.19 to 4.21, that belong to neither and carry no current. Nothing can be drawn through it. A pantograph that crosses it with the motors taking current arcs at the gap, and the arc trips the substation behind it. The boards that guard it are black squares with a white bar: the section board, its bar across the line, ninety metres before the gap in each direction, at 4.10 running Down and 4.30 running Up; and the resume board, its bar along the line, ninety metres beyond it, at 4.30 running Down and 4.10 running Up.

Rule D 24Neutral sections

Shut off power at the section board and coast; take power again only after the resume board. Entering the neutral section with power applied is an incident.

  1. Choose your speed before the section board. Running Down you are on the 8‰ climb: a car coasting up it from 25 km/h will be at walking pace by the resume board, so be at line speed, or near it, when you shut off. Running Up you are on the fall and speed looks after itself.
  2. At the section board, controller to 0. Leave the reverser where it is and the brake alone; you are coasting, not stopping.
  3. Watch the line lamp. It goes dark as the pantograph enters the gap and lights again as it leaves. Two hundred metres of coasting at 50 km/h is a quarter of a minute.
  4. At the resume board, with the lamp lit, notch up again.

The section board stands ninety metres before the gap because a driver takes a moment to act and a train takes a moment to stop drawing current; ninety metres at line speed is six seconds, which is enough. The resume board stands the same distance beyond it so that a train taking power again is doing so with its whole pantograph on the live wire, not on the edge of it. The lamp in the cab is the proof of both.

4.9 Whistle boards and crossings

Millers' Crossing, a farm crossing at post 4.7, is the first road to cross the rails on the Railway. It has no gates and no lamps; it has you. A whistle board, a black W on an ivory disc, stands two hundred metres before it in each direction, at 4.50 running Down and 4.90 running Up, and Rule R 18 now asks for one long blast there, for the crossing beyond it. The long blast is the same key as the short one, held: keep H down for a second and a half or more, and let the Messages tab confirm One long blast. A short blast does not satisfy the board, however many you give; pass the crossing without a long one and the Incident Book records it as passed without a blast. Sound it as you pass the board; a long blast sounded ten seconds before the board still counts, one sounded after the crossing does not.

Anyone on the crossing hears the train before they can see it, because the whistle board is placed where the line is out of sight of the road behind the shoulder of the hill. The blast is for them, not for you: it is the one piece of signalling on the Railway addressed to people who are not on the train.

The Ahead indicator lists the board as Whistle board · one long blast. Running Down, the board comes forty metres after the resume board of the neutral section: power on, then the horn, in that order, both before post 4.5 is behind you.

4.10 Through stations and the block

Wending is no longer the end of anything. The line runs on from it to Coldwater, and Wending has become a through station with two platform tracks: platform 1 on track 1, where Down trains stop at the board at 3.165, and platform 2 on track 2, where Up trains stop at the board at 3.015. Which track you are given is the Box's decision; the home signal is cleared into it, and the switch indicator at WD A or WD B tells you as you arrive. Every home into a platform shows Caution; every starter out of one, WD 7 (Down from track 1) or WD 4 (Up from track 2), stands at Stop until the Box has what it needs to clear it.

What it needs is the Line Warrant. The single line between two boxes is a block section: section A from Ashgrove to Wending, section B from Wending to Coldwater, and since the branch opened section C from Wending to Fernhollow. Each section has one warrant, and a train enters the section only with it. The box at the far end issues the warrant when the section is clear and no other warrant for it is out; the box at your end clears its starter only with the warrant in hand; and when you have arrived complete at the far end, that box cancels it. One warrant, one train, one section. That is Rule S 28, and it is the reason two trains can be on the line at once and never meet between stations.

You will see the block worked in the messages, and you should read them as you read a signal. Two minutes before your booked time the far box speaks: Line clear for 1002 to Coldwater. Warrant B issued; CW 1 cleared into track 1. Then your own: Warrant B in hand. WD 7 cleared for the 08:12 departure. The starter clears at that moment and not before. When you arrive: Train 1002 arrived complete at Coldwater; warrant B cancelled. If instead you read Waiting for line clear, the section is not yours yet, and the starter will stay at Stop past your booked time; that is not lateness of your making and it is not a reason to move. Depart on the starter and the baton, as always, and on nothing else.

A signal at Stop protects the length of track you can see. The warrant protects the length you cannot: three kilometres of single line with a colleague somewhere on it, coming the other way. The starter is tied to the warrant so that the one thing a driver can see, the signal, always agrees with the one thing that matters, the section.

Mind the crossing. When two trains are booked to pass at Wending they arrive from opposite ends into opposite platforms and leave, at the same minute, in opposite directions. Your colleague will be standing across the loop from you while you both wait for the batons, each of you with a warrant for the section the other has just left. Look before you look away: a car in platform 2 tells you section B is clear, and a Box that has not yet said arrived complete tells you it is not. Neither of you departs until both starters are cleared for you, and Mr Pell will show the baton to each of you by name.

4.12 Multiple working

Two Class 1 cars couple to each other as any two vehicles do, coupler on contact and pipe and control line by hand (§ 6). Once the control line is connected the pair is one train, driven from one cab: the controller in the leading cab works both cars' motors, the brake handle works both cars' brakes, the doors key works every door, and the lights the leading cab calls for appear at the ends of the train, head at the front, tail at the rear, nothing at the coupled ends between. This is multiple working, and it is how a two-car train is worked on the Railway.

Rule D 26Multiple working

Two Class 1 cars coupled with pipe and control line connected work as one train from one cab, both pantographs up, both cars' motors answering the controller; the other car's cabs show the lights the leading cab calls for, head at the leading end, tail at the trailing end, nothing at coupled ends. Prove the brake after every coupling.

The other car needs a driver in it only to be prepared: both pantographs go up, both parking brakes come off, and then its cabs are left alone; a colleague may ride in it, and on Duty 401 one does. From the leading cab the pair behaves like a heavier Lark. Notch 3 takes it up the 12‰ as notch 2 takes one car; from 50 km/h a Full application stops it in the same hundred metres, because there is twice the brake for twice the mass; the doors take a second longer to close, and the train is 44 metres long, which on a 160-metre platform is nothing, but at a stop board is 22 metres more to bring to rest exactly.

One driver, one handle, one train: the control line exists so that a two-car train has one will. The rear car's cabs follow the leading cab's lights for the same reason the coach's lamps do, so that the train shows what it is from both ends without anybody walking. The brake is proved after every coupling because a control line can be connected and a pipe forgotten, and the proof is the only way to know.

Which cab leads is not always the one you expect. A pair that arrived at a terminus with car 1004 leading leaves it with car 1001 leading, because the train has turned round and the cars have not. Before you drive, look at which car stands at the leading end. If it is the other one, walk to it: its cab is now yours.

4.13 Splitting and joining

A pair from Ashgrove is one train as far as Wending and two trains beyond it: one car to Coldwater on the main line, the other to Fernhollow on the branch. Coming back, the two cars meet at Wending from opposite valleys and become one train again for Ashgrove. The first is a split and the second a join; each is a coupling operation of § 6 done under the Box's signals in a platform, with a colleague on the other side of it.

Rule D 28Splitting and joining

To split: stop at the board, secure the cab, uncouple at the coupling (pipe first, then the coupler), set TAIL on the new rear cab of your portion, and return to your cab; the other portion's driver takes their cab. To join: the standing portion waits with its train brake applied; the arriving car is called on at shunting speed and makes contact at 2 km/h or less; its driver connects the pipe and control line; the train is proved from the leading cab before it leaves. Portions leave a station in the order they stand: the one nearest the exit first.

Splitting at Wending

  1. Arrive on platform 1 under WD 1 and stop at the board at 3.165 with the whole pair on the platform. Doors as usual.
  2. Secure the cab: controller 0, brake Full, reverser Neutral (Rule R 22). Leave it.
  3. Walk to the coupling between the cars and uncouple: the pipe parts, both portions brake themselves, then the coupler is released (§ 6.2, Rule D 12).
  4. Your portion has a new rear end: the cab that was coupled. Enter it and set its lights to Tail. A car that leaves a split showing nothing at its rear is a car nobody can see the end of; the Incident Book says so.
  5. Walk back to your driving cab and enter it. The colleague in the other car takes their cab at the same time.
  6. Wait for the starter WD 7 and Mr Pell's baton, shown to you by car number. Read WD J's indicator: for Coldwater it lies straight. Your colleague follows two minutes later with WD 7 cleared again and WD J lying for the branch; the Box will not clear it until you are past the junction and the route is released.

Joining at Wending

  1. Arrive on platform 2 under WD 8 and stop at the board at 3.015. Brake Full and leave it there: you are the standing portion, and nothing about you moves until the train is one again.
  2. The Box gives Fernhollow line clear for your colleague and clears WD 10 on its subsidiary: a call-on, Rule S 30. The car comes down the branch, over the junction with WD J lying for it, and onto platform 2 behind you at shunting speed, prepared to stop short of you.
  3. It makes contact at 2 km/h or less and couples (Rule D 10). Your colleague connects the pipe and the control line at the coupling; you will see the pipe lamp go from parted to through.
  4. Prove the brake from your cab (§ 6.3, Rule D 14). The proof reaches the far end of the other car; until it does, Mr Pell keeps the baton in his pocket.
  5. Wait for WD 4 and the baton, then take the pair home. The other car's cabs follow yours; you have nothing to set at its far end.

The order portions leave. At a split the portion nearest the exit goes first, because it is in the way of the other. Which car that is depends on which end of the pair it stands at, and that changes each time the pair turns round at a terminus: on Duty 401 your car leads out of Ashgrove and goes first at Wending, but the other pair, having turned at Ashgrove, arrives at Wending with its branch car at the Down end, and that car leaves first, for Fernhollow, before its partner can leave for Coldwater. The working timetable in Book T is written knowing this; read it before you assume.

A train that is split is two trains with one timetable path each, and a train that is joined is one path made of two. Splitting lets a single car serve a hamlet the whole pair could not fill, and joining lets both come home as one train through the busiest section of the line. The call-on exists because a join is the one occasion on which a train is sent, on purpose, into a platform that already has a train in it; it is done under a subsidiary at shunting speed so that the whole procedure is a coupling and nothing more.

§ 5 Changing ends

At the end of the line the train does not turn round; you do. The ritual is the same on the Class 1 car and on a light locomotive, and once it is in your hands you will do it without thinking. Until then, do it in this order.

  1. Stop at the board. Handle to Full. If there are passengers, work the doors now and close them again.
  2. Secure the cab: controller to 0, brake Full, reverser Neutral (Rule R 22). On a gradient, parking brake On as well (Rule D 22); at the stations, which are level, it is not needed.
  3. Lights at this end to Tail. This end is about to become the rear of the train.
  4. Leave the cab C. You are on the ground.
  5. Walk to the other cab. This is the twenty seconds we spoke of in § 1.
  6. Enter the cab C. The desk is dark except for the pipe gauge and the Line lamp, because the reverser is in Neutral. If you applied the parking brake at the other end, take it Off now, first thing.
  7. Lights at this end to Head.
  8. Reverser to Forward. Remember that Forward is away from the cab you are now sitting in, which is the direction you came from. The train has not turned; your idea of forward has.
  9. Wait for the departure conditions (§ 4.1), horn, and go.

If you forget step 3 you will discover it at the far end, when the Incident Book shows Incorrect lights on the main line and you are looking at a white lamp on the back of your own train. Set the tail lamps before you leave the cab, every time, and the thought will never trouble you.

Forward is relative to the cab and not to the track because a driver's body knows which way it is facing better than it knows which way is Down. When you sit at a desk and push the reverser to Forward, the train goes where you are looking. That is the only convention that survives a tired crew at the end of a long turn.

§ 6 Coupling, uncoupling and the brake test

The Meridian automatic coupler engages by itself when two vehicles touch. Everything else about joining and parting a train is done by a person standing at the coupling, deliberately, with the train held still.

6.1 Coupling

Rule D 10Coupling

Approach the standing vehicle at 5 km/h or less over the last twenty metres. Make contact at 2 km/h or less. The coupler engages mechanically on contact. Then go to the coupling and connect the brake pipe.

  1. You will be under a ground signal showing Shunt, on a route into an occupied track; the station master has cleared it for you to couple. Read the switch indicator at the outer switch as you come to it from the stub: it should lie straight, for track 1 (§ 4.6). Shunting speed is 15 km/h until the last twenty metres. The coach you are making for stands at the platform between posts 3.1 and 3.2 at Wending, between 0.1 and 0.2 at Ashgrove.
  2. At twenty metres, be at 5 km/h or under. Handle between step 1 and Release, controller at 0. The Ahead indicator counts the metres down.
  3. Let the train roll the last few metres at a walking pace and touch at 2 km/h or less. You will feel the coupler take.
  4. Handle to Full. Secure the cab and leave it.
  5. Walk to the coupling. Connect the brake pipe. The panel shows the pipe joined through.
  6. Walk back to the cab, enter it, and run the brake test (§ 6.3) before anything else.

Contact between 2 and 5 km/h is a rough coupling and goes in the Incident Book. Contact above 5 km/h damages the coupler faces and the passengers' opinion of you. If you are coming in too fast, a step-3 application at ten metres will stop the train short; couple again from rest with a notch 1 nudge.

6.2 Uncoupling

Rule D 12Uncoupling

Only when stationary with the train brake applied. At the coupling, first disconnect the brake pipe; the brakes on both parts apply themselves. Then part the coupler.

  1. Stop with the handle in Full. Secure the cab and leave it.
  2. Walk to the coupling between the vehicles you mean to part.
  3. Disconnect the brake pipe. The pipe vents on both sides; you will hear it. The coach is now held by its own brake and will stay where it is.
  4. Part the coupler. The two vehicles are separate.
  5. Walk to the cab of the vehicle that is to move. From here on you are a light locomotive under the station master's ground signals, described in Book S, and every move you make crosses a switch: read its indicator before each one (§ 4.6).

Where the headshunts are: at Wending beyond post 3.2, ending at the buffer stop at 3.3; at Ashgrove before post 0.1, ending at 0.0. Eighty metres each, and a locomotive is sixteen.

The pipe comes off before the coupler because a coach parted with its pipe still charged is a coach with its brakes released and nothing holding it. The order is not a preference; it is what keeps a coach on the platform.

6.3 The brake continuity test

Rule D 14Brake continuity

After coupling, before departure, prove the brake through the train from the cab. Do not depart until the test reports the pipe continuous to the far end.

Press Brake test with the train stationary and the handle in Full. The test releases the pipe, confirms that pressure rises at the far end of the train, applies again, and confirms that it falls there too. It takes about twenty seconds and the Test lamp burns until it is done. If the pipe is connected at every coupling, the panel reports brake continuity proved. If it is not, the report names the coupling, and you have a walk ahead of you.

A brake test is required after every coupling, including the second coupling of a run-round at the far end of the line, even though you connected the same pipe to the same coach an hour ago. Departing without one is recorded.

§ 7 Stabling

A duty ends the way it began, with a vehicle standing quiet at a platform, ready for the next driver to find it exactly as this Book describes in § 3.

  1. Stop at the board and hold the train in Full.
  2. Doors: open for the passengers, then Closed.
  3. Controller 0, reverser Neutral. The cab is secured.
  4. Parking brake On. A stabled vehicle is held by its parking brake, level or not (Rule D 22).
  5. Lights at this end Off.
  6. Pantograph Down. The Line lamp goes out and the cab goes dark.
  7. Leave the cab, walk to the other end, enter it, lights Off. On a locomotive and coach the coach's lamps go out with the pantograph; only the locomotive's own ends need you.
  8. Leave the cab. The duty is complete.

The train brake is left applied too, and the pipe will hold its air for hours; but it is the parking brake that holds the vehicle, because air leaks and a screw does not. The next driver releases the parking brake first and proves the air brake next, which is why § 3 puts those steps where it does.

The Lark is light and willing. From rest, notch 2 gets it rolling; notch 4 will take it to 50 km/h in roughly twenty-five seconds on the level, which is quicker than the timetable needs. A gentle start in notch 1 and 2 to the end of the platform, then notch 3, is comfortable and only a few seconds slower.

From 50 km/h a Full application stops it in about a hundred metres. Step 2 takes nearer two hundred. Those two figures are the whole of the braking arithmetic on this line: with the Ahead indicator reading 150 metres to the board, step 2 is right and step 3 is a little early.

On the 12‰ valley grade gravity asks 4.5 kN of the car. Notch 1 just holds 50 km/h on the climb, notch 2 holds it with something to spare, and the timetable is written for notch 2. Coming down, the car gathers speed coasting; a step 1 application checks it and step 2 slows it (§ 4.5). Left with its brakes released on the grade it is at walking pace in fifteen seconds, which is why the parking brake exists.

The car is short enough that a change of ends is quick, twenty seconds of walking and a few more for the steps. On Duty 101 the six-minute dwell is generous; use the spare for the doors.

On the 8‰ to Coldwater the car has more in hand than on the 12‰, and the only new thing it asks of you is the neutral section at post 4.2 (§ 4.8): be at line speed when you shut off at 4.1, and the coast to 4.3 costs you nothing. Car 1003 is the same car in every particular; when Ms Farrow brings it into the other platform at Wending, what you see across the loop is your own car with somebody else's habits in it.

8.2 The cab

The desk is the standard desk of Fig. 2.1, set across the full width of the cab with the driver's seat on the left. The lights switch on the Class 1 controls the two lamps in the cab front below the windscreen. The doors key opens both doors on both sides; on a platform, only the platform side will actually open.

The pantograph is in the middle of the car, and its lamp shows in both cabs. If you lower it from one cab and walk to the other, you will find it down when you get there. There is one pantograph and one state.

8.3 In multiple

Coupled to another Lark with the control line connected, the car does whatever the leading cab tells it: its motors take the notch, its brakes take the handle, its doors take the key, and its lamps take the lights switch, head or tail according to which end of the train it is. Its own cabs are dark and its own controls are dead until it is uncoupled, when they wake at once, with the reverser in Neutral, the controller at 0 and the brake in Full, exactly as a secured cab is left. Both pantographs stay up throughout: the car takes its own power and lends its own motors. On the 12‰ a pair asks 9 kN of gravity and has 90 kN to answer it with; on the branch's 10‰ a single car climbs with 45 kN against 3.7. Nothing about the Lark changes in multiple except that there is twice as much of it.

The Class 4, the Heron, is a short, double-cabbed electric locomotive built to pull anything the Railway owns. On Duty 201 it pulls one coach, which it barely notices.

9.1 Performance

The Heron is strong and light on its feet. Running alone during a run-round it will reach shunting speed in a couple of seconds on notch 1; treat notch 2 as the ceiling for a light engine inside station limits, and brake early, because sixty-four tonnes have their own opinion about stopping. With one Type C4 coach behind it, notch 2 is plenty for the line: it will reach 50 km/h before the 50 board and hold it on the level in notch 1. Notch 3 and 4 exist for the day it has ten coaches.

Braking: from 50 km/h with one coach, step 2 at about 120 metres from the board. Alone, step 2 from 80 metres at shunting speed is more than enough; step 1 at 15 km/h will stop it within a car length.

The valley grade means little to the Heron. On 12‰ the locomotive and coach together, 94 tonnes, are pulled back with 11 kN, and notch 1 at line speed gives twice that: the climb is made in notch 2 without noticing it. Coming down with the coach, the train is heavier and quicker to gather speed than the Class 1 car; a step 1 application will not hold it on the 12‰ and step 2 is the working position (§ 4.5). Alone, on a level station, none of this applies, but a light locomotive stopped on the grade is 64 tonnes that will move by itself: parking brake before you leave the cab.

A light locomotive approaching a coach to couple should be in notch 0 with the brake between step 1 and Release from twenty metres out. The last two metres it does on its own weight. If it stops a metre short, notch 1 for one second and Release.

9.2 The cab

The standard desk, with a side door to the ground so that a run-round does not mean climbing through the machine room. The lights switch controls the lamps at this end; when a coach is coupled at the other end, that end is left at Off, and the coach shows the tail.

§ 12 Glossary

Advance speed board
A black numeral on an ivory triangle, point up, announcing a lower limit one warning distance ahead. It gives the figure; the speed board beyond it gives the place.
Branch
The Fernhollow branch: the line that leaves the main line at the junction switch WD J and climbs to Fernhollow, with its own kilometrage lettered F, 40 km/h line speed and a 10‰ climb. § 4.11.
Baton
The station master's hand-held disc, green on one face and red on the other. Green shown steadily means ready to start.
Brake pipe
The air pipe running the length of the train. Charged, the brakes release; vented, they apply.
Call-on
A home signal's subsidiary lit for a train: the platform ahead is occupied; pass at shunting speed prepared to stop short of the vehicles, and couple to them only as the Box has instructed. Used for a join. § 4.13, Rule S 30.
Cab A, Cab B
The two driving positions of a two-cab vehicle. Forward is always away from the cab you occupy.
Continuity
The condition of the brake pipe being connected from the cab to the far end of the train, proved by the brake test.
Distant signal
A two-lamp signal with an ivory chevron plate, one warning distance before a home signal and named after it with the letter D, as WD 1D. Caution: the home may be at Stop, slow so as to stop at it. Clear: a clear run through the station. Never at Stop. § 4.7, Rule S 24.
Down, Up
Down is the direction of increasing kilometrage, Ashgrove towards Wending and Coldwater, and on the branch away from Wending towards Fernhollow. Up is the opposite.
Dwell
The time a train stands at a station between arrival and departure.
Facing move
A move that enters a switch at its toe and is sent one way or the other by it. The switch indicator tells you which. § 4.6.
Gradient post
A slate post at every change of grade, with a small ivory disc and two black arms along the line, each tilted with the grade on its side and lettered in per mille; L for level. The arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. Information, not instruction.
Headshunt
The short dead-end track beyond the inner switch of a station loop, into which a locomotive draws to begin a run-round.
Hectometre post
A post every hundred metres on the left of the line in the Down direction, its ivory plate reading the distance from the Ashgrove buffer stop in kilometres and tenths, as 1.4. A position reference, not a signal.
Home signal
The main signal that governs entry to a station from the line.
Join
The making of one train from two portions at a station: the second is called on behind the first, couples, connects, and the whole is proved from the leading cab. § 4.13, Rule D 28.
Junction
Where two lines meet at a switch: at Wending, WD J at 3.36, straight for Coldwater and diverging for Fernhollow, read by its switch indicator and the Box's message. § 4.11.
Kilometre post
The larger post at every full kilometre, the hectometre plate on a larger scale with a green rim, reading the kilometre with its zero, as 2.0. Kilometrage counts from the Ashgrove buffer stop, km 0.0, to the Coldwater buffer stop, km 6.4.
Line Warrant
The single authority for a train to enter a block section. Issued by the box at the far end when the section is clear and no other warrant for it is out; the starter is cleared only with it in hand; cancelled when the train has arrived complete. One warrant, one train, one section. § 4.10, Rule S 28.
Light locomotive
A locomotive running without vehicles attached.
Limit of Shunt
A board beyond which shunting moves do not go. Book S, Rule S 12.
Line speed
50 km/h, the highest speed permitted between stations.
Multiple working
Two Class 1 cars coupled with pipe and control line connected, driven as one train from one cab, both pantographs up, the other car's lamps following. § 4.12, Rule D 26.
Neutral section
A short length of overhead line between two feeds that carries no current, at post 4.2, from 4.19 to 4.21. Power off at the section board, on again after the resume board. § 4.8, Rule D 24.
Notch
One position of the power controller. There are four above 0.
One Train Working
Working a line that carries a single train and no signals. Rule R 24.
Parking brake
The mechanical brake on a cabbed vehicle, worked from either cab, holding about half the full service force without air. Off before moving; on before leaving a cab on a gradient, and whenever stabled. Rule D 22.
Per mille
Parts per thousand, the Railway's unit for gradients: 12‰ is twelve metres of rise in a thousand metres of line, written ‰.
Portion
One of the trains a split makes, or one of the two that a join makes one of. The portion nearest the exit leaves first.
Resume board
A black square with a white bar along the line, ninety metres beyond the neutral section: power may be taken again. § 4.8.
Run through
To pass over a switch, trailing, when it is set for the other branch, forcing the blades across. It damages the switch and is an incident. Rule S 22.
Run-round
The moves by which a locomotive is taken from one end of its train to the other at a terminus, by way of the loop.
Securing
Controller 0, brake Full, reverser Neutral, and on a gradient or when stabling the parking brake, before leaving a cab. Rule R 22.
Section board
A black square with a white bar across the line, ninety metres before the neutral section: shut off power. § 4.8.
Shunting move
Any movement made under a ground signal within station limits, at 15 km/h or less.
Split
The making of two trains from one at a station: secure, uncouple, Tail on the new rear cab, back to the cab; each portion then leaves under its own signal and baton. § 4.13, Rule D 28.
Stabled
Standing out of service: pantograph down, lights off, doors closed, train brake applied, parking brake on.
Starting signal
The main signal at the departure end of a platform that governs leaving the station.
Station limits
The area from the speed board or home signal to the buffer stop, where 25 km/h applies.
Stop board
The board at which a train stops at a platform, front of the train level with the bar.
Switch indicator
A black box on a short post at the toe of every switch, a white bar on each face lying the way the switch lies: along the track for the straight route, leaning towards the diverging track for the diverging route. Read from every leg before crossing. Book S, Rule S 22.
Subsidiary
A small black box with two white lamps, fixed on the post directly under the head of some main signals, so that head and subsidiary read as one signal. At the starters WD 4 and WD 7, lit diagonally with the main at Stop they show Shunt: a shunting move may pass at shunting speed as far as the route goes; a train may not. At the homes WD 8 and WD 10 the same lamps are a call-on for a train. Book S, Rules S 10 and S 30.
Tail, Head
Red lamps at the rear of a train; white lamps at its front.
Through station
A station the line runs through rather than ends at, with a platform track for each direction: Wending, platform 1 on track 1 for Down trains and platform 2 on track 2 for Up trains. § 4.10.
Trailing move
A move that enters a switch along one of its branches and leaves through the toe. Safe only when the switch is set for that branch; otherwise a run through. § 4.6.
Whistle board
A black W on an ivory disc, two hundred metres before a crossing: sound one long blast, the horn held a second and a half or more. At 4.50 and 4.90 for Millers' Crossing. § 4.9, Rule R 18.
Warning distance
The distance from an advance speed board, or a distant signal, to the speed board or home it announces: 200 m where the approach is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more, enough to lose 25 km/h at 0.5 m/s² with fifty metres to spare. On this line 200 m at 2.65 and 250 m at 0.70.
