The fourth edition gives every switch a switch indicator at its toe, a black box whose white bar lies the way the switch lies, and adds Rule S 22, which forbids running through a switch that shows set against the move. The station tables, the run-round example and the glossary follow. No rule was renumbered.

§ 3.3 Switch indicators New section

Why: the switches now carry an indicator, and a driver needs to know how to read it from the toe and from a branch.

Every switch on the Railway carries a switch indicator at its toe: a black box on a short post, standing on the side of the line away from the diverging track, with a white bar on each of its two faces. The bar lies the way the switch lies. Along the track, the switch is set for the straight route, which on this line is always track 1 (normal). Leaning towards the diverging track, it is set for the diverging route, track 2 (reverse). The box has a face towards the toe and a face towards the branches, so it can be read from every leg of the switch.

Fig. 3.2 shows the two states seen from the toe, with the switch each reports sketched beneath: SET STRAIGHT, the bar upright; SET DIVERGING, the bar leaning towards the diverging track.

Read it from wherever you are coming from. From the toe (a facing move) the bar tells you which route the switch will take you onto: straight into track 1, or leaning off into track 2. From a branch (a trailing move, as when you set back from the headshunt onto track 2 during a run-round) the same bar tells you whether the switch is set for the track you are on or against you: if the bar leans towards your track, or lies straight and your track is the straight one, the switch is for you; otherwise it is set against you, and the points would be forced open by your wheels if you went on. The cab's list of what lies ahead names the next switch and reads its indicator for you in the same words: Switch WD B · set for you, or SET AGAINST YOU.

Why we do this. A route's signal promises that the switches are set and locked, and the frame makes that promise hard to break. But a promise is worth more when it can be checked, and the driver is the one person who is actually at the switch. The indicator lets them confirm, in one glance, that the points lie as the signal said; on a trailing move it is the last check before the wheels reach the blades. Running through a switch set against the move bends the blades and their rodding, puts the switch out of use until the Engineer has been, and can derail the tail of the very move that did it. We would rather spend a lamp on every switch than a morning on any of that.

Rule S 22 · Switch indicators. Before moving over a switch, read its indicator. Never run through a switch that shows set against your move: stop and speak to the Box. Running through switches is an incident.

Note. A switch indicator is not a signal. It carries no authority to move and none to stop: the signal governs the move, and Rule S 14 still applies in full. The indicator tells you how the switch lies, which is a different question, and one you should always be able to answer before your wheels reach the points.

§ 7.3 A worked example: the run-round at Wending Wording changed

Why: the driver now reads each switch indicator before moving over the switch, and the example shows where.

Into the headshunt. … WD 3 changes to SHUNT. The locomotive draws forward at shunting speed through WD B into the headshunt …

Round the loop. … WD 6 and WD 4 change to SHUNT. The locomotive runs Up through WD B onto track 2, along track 2 past the standing coach, passes WD 4 at SHUNT, through WD A onto the stub, and stops short of the Limit of Shunt board …

Onto the train. … WD 5 changes to SHUNT although track 1 is occupied. The locomotive runs Down through WD A onto track 1 …

Into the headshunt. … WD 3 changes to SHUNT. The driver reads WD B's indicator from track 1: the bar lies straight, the switch is for them. The locomotive draws forward at shunting speed through WD B into the headshunt …

Round the loop. … WD 6 and WD 4 change to SHUNT. Before setting back, the driver reads WD B's indicator from the headshunt: the bar leans towards track 2, which is where the move is going (Rule S 22). The locomotive runs Up through WD B onto track 2, along track 2 past the standing coach, passes WD 4 at SHUNT, reads WD A's indicator leaning towards track 2 as it trails through onto the stub, and stops short of the Limit of Shunt board …

Onto the train. … WD 5 changes to SHUNT although track 1 is occupied. The driver reads WD A's indicator from the toe: the bar lies straight, so the switch will take the locomotive onto track 1 and not back onto track 2. The locomotive runs Down through WD A onto track 1 …

§ 10 Glossary Glossary

Why: four new terms.

Facing move — A movement over a switch from its toe, which the switch may send one way or the other. The indicator shows which.

Run through — To pass over a switch from a branch when the switch is set for the other branch, forcing the blades open. Damages the switch, can derail the tail of the move, and is always recorded (Rule S 22).

Switch indicator — A black box on a short post at the toe of every switch, with a white bar on each face that lies the way the switch lies: along the track for the straight route, leaning towards the diverging track for the diverging route. Read from every leg. Not a signal.

Trailing move — A movement over a switch from one of its branches towards the toe. The switch must be set for that branch; its indicator shows whether it is.
