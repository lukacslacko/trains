Book R, second edition (September 2026) → third edition (September 2026). Issued 9 September 2026. Paste inside the back cover of a second-edition copy, or read it here to bring yourself up to date.

The third edition gives the line its profile. The valley climbs 12‰ from Ashgrove towards Wending; gradient posts mark the changes of grade; the warning distance before a speed board now depends on whether the approach falls; and Rule R 22 asks for the parking brake where a vehicle stands on a gradient or is left. Two driving rules, D 20 and D 22, are cited from Book D. Nothing was renumbered.

§ 2 Terms and directions Glossary

The definition of "Warning distance" now carries the two distances, and four terms the new § 2.2 and the amended rules rely on are added after it.

Warning distance. The distance from an advance speed board to the speed board it announces: 200 m on the Ashgrove–Wending line. It is enough to lose the difference in speed at a steady service deceleration with a margin for reaction, fog and snow.

Warning distance. The distance from an advance speed board to the speed board it announces: 200 m where the approach to the board is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more. It is enough to lose the difference in speed at a steady service deceleration with a margin for reaction, fog and snow (§ 4.1).

Gradient. The slope of the line, given in per mille and named from the Down direction: a rising gradient climbs towards Wending, a falling one descends towards it. What rises for a Down train falls for an Up train. See § 2.2.

Per mille (‰). Metres of rise or fall in every thousand metres of line. A gradient of 12‰ climbs 12 m in a kilometre.

Gradient post. A slate post with a small ivory disc and two black arms, standing at every change of grade. Each arm points along the line on its side, tilted with the grade there and lettered with it in per mille, L for level. The arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. Information only; it carries no instruction.

Parking brake. The hand-applied brake of a cabbed vehicle, independent of the air. It holds a vehicle standing on any grade on this Railway, and it is what secures a vehicle that is left (Rule R 22, Rule D 22).

§ 2.2 The profile New section

The line was described as if it were flat. It is not, and the rules that follow turn on where it is not.

The line is not flat. The Wend valley climbs from Ashgrove to Wending, and the railway climbs with it: level through the station limits at Ashgrove, then 12‰ up the valley from post 0.5 to post 2.4, easing to 6‰ towards the weir, and level again through the station limits at Wending from post 2.8. Wending stands 25.2 m above Ashgrove. A Down train climbs all the way; an Up train descends all the way, and arrives at the Ashgrove speed board with the 12‰ fall still under it.

Fig. 2.2, new: the profile of the line with heights exaggerated, the three gradient posts at km 0.5, 2.4 and 2.8, and the heights 22.8 m at post 2.4 and 25.2 m at Wending.

Every change of grade is marked on the ground by a gradient post. It is a post like the hectometre posts, but instead of a plate it carries a small disc with two arms, one pointing along the line each way. Each arm is tilted with the grade on its side and lettered with that grade in per mille, or L where the line is level. Read the arm on the side you are travelling towards: tilted up and away from you, the line ahead rises; down and away, it falls. The post says nothing about what to do. Like the hectometre posts it is information, and the rules that turn on a gradient are written elsewhere: the warning distance in Rule R 10, securing in Rule R 22, and the handling of a train on a grade in Rules D 20 and D 22.

Why we do this: Twelve per mille is a gentle grade as railways go, but gravity on it is a fifth of what the Class 1 car can pull, and enough to set any unbraked vehicle rolling within a few seconds. A driver who knows where the grade begins can start, brake and leave a vehicle accordingly; a driver who does not will learn it from the Incident Book. We would rather they learned it from a post.

Station limits, 25: … An advance speed board 25 stands one warning distance before it.

Station limits, 25: … An advance speed board 25 stands one warning distance before it: 200 m on a level or rising approach, 250 m where the approach falls at 10‰ or more.

Added after the whole-train sentence: "The warning distance before a speed board is 200 m where the approach to it is level or rising, and 250 m where the approach falls at 10‰ or more."

§ 4.1 Where a limit begins and ends Wording changed

The paragraph on the advance board gave one warning distance and one braking figure; it now gives both, and places both advance boards on this line.

On the Ashgrove–Wending line the warning distance is 200 m. At the service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² a train loses the 25 km/h between 50 and 25 in about 145 m; the remaining 55 m are the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the disc cannot be seen until it is close.

The warning distance is 200 m where the approach to the board is level or rising, and 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more, because gravity is braking against the driver on a fall. At the service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² a train loses the 25 km/h between 50 and 25 in about 145 m on the level and about 190 m on a 12‰ fall; the rest of the warning distance is the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the disc cannot be seen until it is close. On this line the Down advance board for Wending stands at km 2.65, 200 m before its speed board, the approach rising at 6‰; the Up advance board for Ashgrove stands at km 0.70, 250 m before its speed board, the approach falling at 12‰.

Rule R 22 Securing Wording changed

The rule had three steps and relied on the air brake alone. It gains a fourth step for the parking brake, asked for where gravity or time could defeat the air.

1. Power controller to 0. 2. Train brake to Full. 3. Reverser to Neutral. The pantograph may stay up. A cab left in any other state is unsecured, and the fact is recorded.

1. Power controller to 0. 2. Train brake to Full. 3. Reverser to Neutral. 4. Parking brake On, where the vehicle stands on a gradient, or is to be left unattended. A vehicle being stabled is always left unattended. The pantograph may stay up. A cab in any other state cannot be left: the door does not open until the cab is secured, and the cab tells you which condition is missing.

§ 7 Securing a vehicle, text and "Why we do this" Wording changed

The paragraph after the rule and the explanation now say when the air alone is enough and when it is not, and point to the driving rules for the grade.

… a vehicle standing at a platform is as capable of rolling as one standing anywhere else.

The brake is applied fully so that nothing rolls. The reverser is put to Neutral … Stabling proper, with the pantograph down and the lights off, is in Book D; this rule is the part of stabling that happens every time.

… a vehicle standing at a platform is as capable of rolling as one standing anywhere else. On the level, and with the driver coming straight back, the air brake alone holds it for the walk; on a gradient, or when the vehicle is to be left, the parking brake holds it whatever the air does. Releasing the parking brake again before moving, and the handling of a train on a grade, are in Rule D 22 and Rule D 20.

The brake is applied fully so that nothing rolls. The reverser is put to Neutral … The parking brake is asked for on a gradient because air leaks and gravity does not: a vehicle held on air alone for long enough on the valley grade will, one day, go to Ashgrove by itself. Stabling proper, with the pantograph down and the lights off, is in Book D; this rule is the part of stabling that happens every time.

§ 10.2 The entries, "Left cab unsecured" Entry withdrawn

The cab door interlock made the entry impossible: a cab that is not secured cannot be left at all, so there is nothing to record.

Left cab unsecured. A cab was left with Rule R 22 not satisfied (R 22).

Withdrawn. In its place Rule R 22 says that the door does not open until the cab is secured and that the cab names the missing condition; the Incident Book no longer has an entry for it.

§ 10.2 The entries Table rows added

Three kinds of entry that only a gradient can produce.

Rolled back on the gradient. A train starting on a rise moved more than half a metre against the direction set (D 20).

Ran away. A vehicle moved with no direction set: the reverser in Neutral, or no one in the cab (D 22).

Moved with the parking brake applied. A train moved more than a few metres with a parking brake still on (D 22).

§ 11 The rules in one page Wording changed

The pin-up card's R 10 and R 22 lines carry the new distances and the parking brake; D 20 and D 22 are added to the D rules it lists.

R 10: … higher only once the rear has passed it.

R 22: Before leaving the cab: power 0, brake Full, reverser Neutral.

R 10: … higher only once the rear has passed it. Advance board 200 m before a reduction, 250 m on a fall of 10‰ or more.

R 22: Before leaving the cab: power 0, brake Full, reverser Neutral; parking brake On on a gradient or when leaving the vehicle.

D 20: Starting on a rise: hold on the train brake, apply power, release as the traction takes up. No rolling back.

D 22: Parking brake: off before moving, on before leaving the cab on a gradient and whenever stabled.
