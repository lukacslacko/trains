§ 4.2 Speed boards and advance speed boards New section

Why: the rule of application and the advance board deserve a page of their own.

A speed board says what the speed is from where it stands. It is read against the whole train, not the cab: a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it. So a lower limit applies from the moment the front of the train reaches its board, and a higher limit applies only once the rear of the train has passed its board, because until then the tail is still on the slower stretch (Rule R 10). Leaving station limits, the driver waits for the last vehicle to clear the 50 board before notching up.

An advance speed board says that a lower limit lies ahead. It carries the numeral of the speed board it announces, on a triangle so that it cannot be mistaken for the board itself, and it stands one warning distance before that board: on the Ashgrove–Wending line, 200 m. The driver must be at the announced speed when the front of the train reaches the speed board. Higher limits are not announced; there is nothing to prepare for.

Why we do this. We put the advance board 200 m out because a train at 50 km/h loses the 25 km/h down to 25 in about 145 m at a service deceleration of 0.5 m/s². The rest of the distance is the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the speed board itself is lost in fog or snow. On such a day the driver brakes at the advance board and does not wait to see the board it announces. The distance is the same for the whole line, so that one habit serves everywhere.

Note. Read with the posts: the Down advance board at 2.650 stands between posts 2.6 and 2.7 and its speed board at 2.850 between 2.8 and 2.9; the Up advance board at 0.650 between 0.6 and 0.7 and its speed board at 0.450 between 0.4 and 0.5.
