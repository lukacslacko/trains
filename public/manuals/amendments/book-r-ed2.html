Book R, first edition (September 2026) → second edition (September 2026). Issued 8 September 2026. Paste inside the back cover of a first-edition copy, or read it here to bring yourself up to date.

The second edition makes one change of substance and carries it through the Book: a speed limit applies to the whole train, and every reduction is announced by an advance speed board one warning distance ahead. Nothing was renumbered.

§ 2 Terms and directions Glossary

Two terms the new § 4.1 relies on, added to the definition list after "Station limits".

Advance speed board. A black numeral on an ivory triangle, point up, standing one warning distance before a speed board that lowers the limit. It announces the lower limit; it does not impose it. The board it announces carries the same numeral (§ 4.1, Book S).

Warning distance. The distance from an advance speed board to the speed board it announces: 200 m on the Ashgrove–Wending line. It is enough to lose the difference in speed at a steady service deceleration with a margin for reaction, fog and snow.

Rule R 10 Speeds Wording changed

The "Begins" column of the table said where each limit begins for the front of the train only; it now says which end of the train the board is read against, and names the advance board.

Main line, 50: At the speed board 50 on leaving station limits, until the next speed board 25.

Station limits, 25: At the speed board 25 on the approach, or at the home signal where there is one, and as far as the buffer stop.

Main line, 50: Once the rear of the train has passed the speed board 50 on leaving station limits, until the front reaches the next speed board 25.

Station limits, 25: From the moment the front reaches the speed board 25 on the approach, or the home signal where there is one, and as far as the buffer stop. An advance speed board 25 stands one warning distance before it.

Rule R 10 Speeds Text added

The rule of application, stated once inside the rule so that it carries the rule's authority.

A limit applies to the whole train. A train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it: a reduction applies from the moment the front reaches its board, and a rise only once the rear has passed its board.

§ 4.1 Where a limit begins and ends New section

Explains the whole-train rule, the advance speed board, the warning distance and the figures behind it, with a "Why we do this" box.

A speed board marks a point on the line, but a train is not a point: it is a length, and every part of it is somewhere. So a limit is read against the whole train. Where the limit goes down, the front of the train is the part that reaches the board first, and the whole train must already be at the lower speed when it does. Where the limit goes up, the rear of the train is the last part still in the slow section, and the higher speed may be used only once the rear has passed the board that raises it. The short form is the one in Rule R 10: a train's limit is the lowest limit under any part of it.

A reduction is announced. One warning distance before every speed board that lowers the limit stands an advance speed board: the same numeral, but on an ivory triangle with its point up instead of a disc. It imposes nothing itself; it tells the driver that the disc with that numeral stands one warning distance ahead, and that the train must be at that speed when the front reaches it. On the Ashgrove–Wending line the warning distance is 200 m. At the service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² a train loses the 25 km/h between 50 and 25 in about 145 m; the remaining 55 m are the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the disc cannot be seen until it is close. The boards themselves are drawn in Book S; the way to brake between them is in Book D.

A rise is not announced. Nothing is lost by finding a higher limit a little late, so a speed board that raises the limit stands alone, and the driver reads it when the rear has cleared it.

Why we do this. The rear matters because the tail of a train does not know that the front has passed a board. A coach still in the station limits is still passing the switches, the platform end and whatever stands beside them, at whatever speed the driver has chosen for the front. And a reduction is announced because braking wants a distance, not a moment: with an advance board the driver can begin a steady, well-defined application and arrive at the lower speed exactly where it is required, in clear weather or in fog and snow alike, instead of guessing where the disc will appear and braking hard when it does.
