§ 4.2 Speed boards and advance speed boards Wording changed

Why: the warning distance is now set by the grade of the approach (Rule R 10).

…it stands one warning distance before that board: on the Ashgrove–Wending line, 200 m.

Why we do this. We put the advance board 200 m out because a train at 50 km/h loses the 25 km/h down to 25 in about 145 m at a service deceleration of 0.5 m/s². The rest of the distance is the driver's allowance … The distance is the same for the whole line, so that one habit serves everywhere.

Note. … the Up advance board at 0.650 between 0.6 and 0.7 and its speed board at 0.450 between 0.4 and 0.5.

…it stands one warning distance before that board. The warning distance is set by the grade of the approach: 200 m where the line up to the speed board is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more (Rule R 10).

Why we do this. We put the advance board 200 m out because a train at 50 km/h loses the 25 km/h down to 25 in about 145 m at a service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² on the level. On a 12‰ fall gravity takes back a quarter of that deceleration and the same braking needs about 190 m, so there the board stands 250 m out. What is left over in either case is the driver's allowance for reaction, and for the days when the speed board itself is lost in fog or snow. On such a day the driver brakes at the advance board and does not wait to see the board it announces. Two distances, chosen by the grade, are still few enough for one habit: triangle, then brake.

Note. Read with the posts: the Down advance board at 2.650 stands between posts 2.6 and 2.7 and its speed board at 2.850 between 2.8 and 2.9, with the last 6‰ of the climb between them; the Up advance board at 0.700 stands at post 0.7 and its speed board at 0.450 between 0.4 and 0.5, with the foot of the 12‰ fall and the gradient post at 0.5 between them.

§ 4.4 Gradient posts New section · Fig. 4.3

Why: the line now has a profile, and a sign to mark it. The contents page lists the new section.

The Wend valley climbs. From the Ashgrove buffer stop the line is level through station limits, rises at 12‰ from km 0.500 to 2.400, eases to 6‰ as far as 2.800, and is level again through Wending, which stands 25.2 m above Ashgrove. Down trains climb; Up trains descend. Every place where the grade changes is marked by a gradient post: a slate post carrying a small ivory disc at the top and two black arms, one reaching along the line each way. Each arm is tilted with the grade on its own side of the post and is lettered with that grade in per mille, or L where the line is level.

Fig. 4.3 shows the gradient post at km 0.5 as a Down train sees it: a level arm to the left lettered L, an arm rising to the right lettered 12‰, on a slate post with an ivory disc at the crossing. Caption: "The gradient post at km 0.5 as a Down train sees it: level behind, 12‰ rising ahead. An Up train reads the same two arms from the other side."

Read the post from wherever you are coming from: the arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. If that arm is tilted up and away from you, the line rises ahead; tilted down and away, it falls; level, it is level. The other arm tells you what you have just left. So the post at km 0.5 says to a Down train "level behind you, 12‰ up ahead", and to an Up train, reading the same arms from the other side, "12‰ down behind you, level ahead". The figures are the same from either side; only the sense of the tilt changes with the way you face, which is why the post needs no lettering for direction.

A gradient post is information, not instruction. Nothing about it tells a driver what speed to run or where to stop; it tells them what the line will do to the train, so that they can brake earlier on a fall, hold a train on a rise before releasing it, and know why the motors are working harder than the speedometer suggests. Book D gives the driving; Book P gives the profile the posts mark, drawn to scale.

Rule S 20 Wording changed

Why: gradient posts, like the other posts, carry no instruction.

Rule S 20 Posts. Hectometre and kilometre posts are a position reference and carry no instruction. Quote them when you report where a train, a fault or a person is.

Rule S 20 Posts and gradient posts. Hectometre posts, kilometre posts and gradient posts are a position and profile reference and carry no instruction. Quote the posts when you report where a train, a fault or a person is; read the gradient posts to know what the line does ahead.

§ 10 Glossary Glossary

Why: two new terms, and the warning distance is no longer one number.

Warning distance — The distance between an advance speed board and the speed board it announces: 200 m on the Ashgrove–Wending line.

Gradient post — A slate post with a small ivory disc and two black arms at every change of grade, each arm tilted with the grade on its side and lettered in per mille (L for level). The arm on the side you are travelling towards shows the grade ahead. Information only.

Per mille — The Railway's unit of gradient, ‰: metres of rise or fall in each kilometre. The valley grade is 12‰, that is 12 m in every kilometre.

Warning distance — The distance between an advance speed board and the speed board it announces: 200 m where the approach is level or rising, 250 m where it falls at 10‰ or more. On the Ashgrove–Wending line, 250 m at Ashgrove and 200 m at Wending.
