§ 1 What the permanent way is

Everything in the other Books happens on top of something. This Book is about that something: the ground that was shaped, the stone that was laid on it, and the steel that was fixed to the stone. We call it the permanent way because it was built to stay.

A train is only ever as good as the metre of track it is standing on.Engineer's Office, first survey report

Read from the bottom up, the permanent way is five things laid one on the other.

Formation
The prepared ground: a level or evenly graded bed, cut out of the hillside or built up from it, wide enough for the track and a walking cess on each side, and shaped so that water runs off it rather than into it.
Drainage
The ditches, pipes and culverts that take that water away. A cess drain runs along each side of the formation. Every culvert under the line has a number and a mouth the Engineer can look into.
Ballast
Broken hard stone, angular so that the pieces lock together, laid in a bed that holds the sleepers in place, spreads the load of a train into the formation, and lets rain fall straight through.
Sleepers
The crossmembers that hold the two rails at their distance apart and carry the load into the ballast. Ours are concrete, heavy on purpose.
Rails and fastenings
The two steel rails the wheels run on, and the clips and pads that hold each rail to each sleeper at exactly the right gauge and inclination.

The Engineer cares about water before anything else. Steel wears slowly and stone settles slowly, but water works fast: it softens the formation, it pumps fine mud up through the ballast until the stone no longer drains, and it lifts the track in frost. Almost every track fault that is not simply age began as a drainage fault. When the Engineer walks the line, the ditches get the first look and the rails the second.

§ 2 The line

One line and a branch, four stations, eight kilometres and a little more. It is still small enough to know every metre of, which is the point.

The Ashgrove–Wending–Coldwater line runs 6.4 km south to north up the Wend valley, from the market town at the foot of the valley, past the mill village by the weir, to the quarry village at its head. It is a single line. In plan the main line is straight for the whole of its length, with the loops at each station laid parallel to the running line at 5 m centres; the Railway's only curve is where the Fernhollow branch leaves it at Wending (§ 2.1). It is electrified throughout at 1.5 kV DC by overhead line, branch included, fed from Ashgrove and from Coldwater; where the two feeds meet, at km 4.2, the line is broken by a neutral section (§ 6). Wending, once the end of the line, is now a through station with a platform on each of its loop tracks and the junction beyond its north switch.

Kilometrage counts from the buffer stop at Ashgrove, km 0.000, to the buffer stop at Coldwater, km 6.400. Down is the direction of increasing kilometrage, Ashgrove towards Wending; Up is the other. The branch keeps its own count, lettered F, from the junction switch to its buffer stop (§ 2.1). The ground is marked every 100 m by a hectometre post, an ivory plate reading the kilometre, a point and the hectometre digit (1.4), and every full kilometre by a kilometre post, which is the same plate on a larger scale, reading the kilometre with its zero (1.0, 2.0, 3.0) in the same black figures and distinguished only by a green rim. Both stand on the left of the line in the Down direction and are moved outward past track 2 where a loop exists. The posts, their lettering and how a position is quoted from them are described in Book S; this Book only insists that they are the Engineer's first instrument, since every fault is reported and every drawing is read by the post nearest to it.

2.1 The junction and the branch

Beyond Wending's north switch the single line runs 140 m to the junction switch WD J at km 3.360, a turnout that chooses between two lines rather than two tracks of one station. Its toe faces the station: a Down train leaving Wending is set either straight on, for Coldwater, or over the switch, for the branch, and the two lines converge at the same switch for Up trains coming the other way. The junction is laid on the level, inside Wending's station limits, so that a train held at WD 8 or WD 10 stands on level track and a portion set back over the junction has no grade to fight. It carries a switch indicator like every other switch, described in Book S.

From the switch the branch curves away from the main line through 25 degrees over its first 100 m, a curve of about 230 m radius, the only curve on the Railway and the reason the branch has a speed of its own. From the end of the curve it runs straight up the side valley for 2.2 km to Fernhollow, a hamlet of fern gatherers and a chapel. The branch keeps its own kilometrage, counted from the junction switch: F 0.000 at WD J, F 2.320 at the Fernhollow buffer stop. Its hectometre posts are the standard plate with the letter F before the figures, F 0.1 to F 2.3, and its kilometre posts read F 1.0 and F 2.0 with the green rim; a position on the branch is quoted with the letter, "stopped at F 1.7", so that no one confuses the two counts. Down on the branch is away from Wending, as on the main line it is away from Ashgrove.

The branch is built to the standards of § 1.1 in every particular but two: its line speed is 40 km/h rather than 50, on account of the curve and the grade, and its rails are laid with a check rail through the curve, since a slow train on a tight curve leans on its outer rail. Fernhollow has no loop and no switches: one platform track, a stop board, a buffer stop, and a home signal and a starter that belong to Fernhollow Box. A car arrives, its driver changes ends, and it leaves the way it came.

We put the junction beyond the station rather than at its throat so that the north stub can be worked as the run-round stub it always was, and so that the two Up homes, WD 8 from Coldwater and WD 10 from Fernhollow, each protect their own line before the switch. The price is 140 m of single line that every northward movement shares, and a Box that must lock the junction for one movement at a time. The Engineer's part of that bargain is simple: the ground under WD J is level, the sighting from both homes to the switch is clear, and the switch is walked and gauged more often than any other on the Railway.

3.1 Reading a gradient

We give gradients in per mille: metres of rise or fall in every thousand metres of line. On the 12‰ section the rails climb 12 m in each kilometre, 1.2 m in each hectometre between the posts, and 26 cm over the length of a Class 1 car. The figure is small enough to say quickly on the telephone and large enough to feel from the cab.

Some railways write the same slope as a ratio, one unit up in so many along. 12‰ is 1 in 83, 10‰ is 1 in 100, 8‰ is 1 in 125, 6‰ is 1 in 167. We prefer the per-mille figure because it adds and compares directly: a 12‰ grade is twice a 6‰ grade, and gravity on it is twice as strong.

3.2 What a gradient does to a train

On a slope, part of a train's weight pulls along the rails instead of into them. The share is the gradient itself: on 12‰ it is twelve thousandths of the weight. For the Class 1 car of Duty 101, 38 t, that is 4.5 kN, about a tenth of what its motors can give; for the Class 4 and its coach, 94 t together, it is 11 kN against a locomotive that can pull 140 kN. Neither notices the climb as effort. Both notice it as a hand on the coupling the moment the brakes come off: a train left unbraked on 12‰ moves, and keeps moving, at once and without a sound.

This is why the driver's rules for starting on a rise and for the parking brake exist, and why they are written in Book D rather than here. The Engineer's part of the bargain is to keep the station limits level, so that the places where trains stand are the places where gravity has nothing to say. The 8‰ of section B asks a little less of a train than the 12‰ of section A, and a little more of the driver descending it, since it is longer: an Up train from Coldwater is on the fall for 2.2 km before the level at Wending. The branch's 10‰ is between the two, 3.7 kN on a Class 1 car; an Up train from Fernhollow is on it for 1.6 km, the steepest fall on the Railway after section A's, and it ends 300 m short of the junction, on the level, where the train must be at 25 km/h and ready for WD 10.

3.3 The branch profile

The branch begins at the junction, level, and stays level for 300 m while the curve straightens out. Then it climbs at 10‰ for 1.6 km, steeper than section B and gentler only than section A, and runs level for the last 420 m into Fernhollow. Its heights are counted, like the main line's, above the Ashgrove buffer stop: it starts at Wending's 25.2 m and ends at 41.2 m, sixteen metres up in a little over two kilometres.

§ 4 Gradient posts

The profile of § 3 is on paper. On the ground it is written on seven small posts, one at each place where the slope changes, five on the main line and two on the branch.

Rule P 10Gradient posts

A gradient post stands at every change of grade. Its arms carry the grade on each side in per mille, L for level; the arm on the side you travel towards shows the grade ahead. Posts carry no instruction.

The post is the standard slate post of the hectometre posts, with a small ivory disc at its head and two black arms fixed to the disc, one reaching along the line each way. Each arm is tilted with the grade on its own side of the post: an arm on a rising section slopes up as it leaves the disc, an arm on a falling section slopes down, and an arm on the level is horizontal. Each arm is lettered with its grade in per mille, and a level arm with the letter L. The post stands on the same side of the line as the hectometre posts, on the left in the Down direction, at the exact kilometre of the change. Dimensions and lettering are given in Book I; the post also appears in the catalogue of boards in Book S, where its place among the things that stand beside the line is fixed.

Reading a post in either direction

Stand on the line and look at the post. The arm pointing the way you are going tells you the grade ahead, and the way it leans tells you which kind: tilted up and away from you, the line rises; down and away, it falls; level, it is level. The arm behind you tells you the grade you have just left, which matters to the rear of a long train that is still on it. A Down train passing post 0.5 reads L on the arm behind and 12‰ rising on the arm ahead; an Up train at the same post reads 12‰ on the arm it has just come down and L on the arm ahead, and the tilt of the 12‰ arm, up and away from the Up train, confirms that what it has just done is descend.

A post at every change of grade, and only there, keeps the ground and the profile in agreement. If a post says 12‰ ahead, the next post is the only place that can say otherwise. A driver who knows the seven posts knows the whole Railway, and a driver on a strange line can learn it from the cab window without a diagram.

§ 5 Speeds and warning distances

Where a speed board or a home signal stands is a question for the Signal Engineer. How far before it the advance board or the distant signal must stand is a question for the profile, and so it is answered here.

Rule P 12Warning distance

The advance speed board stands 200 m before the speed board it announces where the approach is level or rising, and 250 m where the approach falls at 10‰ or more.

The approach is the stretch a train covers while slowing, measured back from the speed board over the warning distance itself. A falling approach lengthens every braking distance, so on a falling approach of 10‰ or more the advance board is set out a further 50 m. Rises to a higher limit carry no advance board, since nothing has to be lost to obey them. The same rule places the distant signals of Book S, which warn of a home signal as the advance board warns of a speed board: each stands one warning distance before its home, by the grade of the approach. The rule is the same on the branch, where the line speed is 40 km/h and the station limits 25 as everywhere: a branch train has 15 km/h to lose rather than 25, but it loses them on a fall as steep as any but section A's, and the rule is written for the fall, not for the speed.

The Up approach to Wending from Coldwater falls at 8‰ for 2.2 km, which is long but not steep: the rule's threshold is 10‰, so the Up advance board and WD 8D stand at the ordinary 200 m. The extra fifty metres are given for the steepness of the fall, not its length, because it is steepness that takes braking away from a train. Wending's north limit and WD 8 moved out to 3.500 and 3.440 when the junction was laid, so that the whole of the junction lies within the 25 km/h limit; the advance board and the distant moved with them.

The Up approach to Wending from Fernhollow falls at exactly 10‰, which is the threshold: the branch's advance board and WD 10D are the first on the Railway to earn the extra fifty metres from a fall of one hundredth. They stand at F 0.390 and F 0.330, on the fall itself, and the boards they announce, at F 0.140 and F 0.080, stand on the level that begins at post F 0.3.

The arithmetic is short. At the Railway's service deceleration of 0.5 m/s² a train loses the 25 km/h between 50 and 25 in about 145 m on the level. Down a 12‰ fall, gravity takes back 0.12 m/s² of that braking, and the same speed comes off in about 190 m. Two hundred metres would leave the Up driver ten metres of margin; two hundred and fifty leaves sixty, which is what the level approach enjoys. The remainder of the warning distance is not the Engineer's; it is the driver's allowance for the moment of reaction, and for fog and snow, when the advance board is braked at without waiting to see the speed board.

The whole-train rule for speed limits, and the conduct of a train between the two boards, are Rule R 10; the method of braking for a reduction is in Book D. This Book only fixes where the boards must stand so that those rules can be kept.

§ 6 Features of the line

Four things on the line are neither track nor signal, and each is the Engineer's to keep: a gap in the wire, a gate across the formation, three lengths of line that must be kept clear for the boxes, and a place where the line divides.

6.1 The neutral section

The overhead line is fed from two ends: a substation at Ashgrove and another at Coldwater. Where the two feeds meet, at km 4.2, they must not be joined, since the two supplies are not in step with each other. The contact wire is therefore broken by an insulated length between km 4.190 and 4.210, the neutral section, which carries no volts at all. A pantograph runs under it as under any other wire, but nothing can be drawn from it, and a train that tries to draw current across the gap arcs at the pantograph, burns the wire at both ends of the gap, and trips the substation behind it.

The Engineer marks the gap for the driver with two pairs of boards: a section board, a black square with a white bar across, 90 m before the gap in each direction (at 4.100 for Down trains and 4.300 for Up trains), and a resume board, a black square with a white bar along the line, 90 m beyond it (at 4.300 for Down trains and 4.100 for Up trains). Between the section board and the resume board the driver shuts off power and coasts; the boards and the driver's conduct are Book D's. The Engineer's part is the gap itself, its insulators, and the 90 m: enough for a Class 1 car at line speed to have its controller closed before the pantograph reaches the wire's end.

Ninety metres is not a braking distance and needs none. It is the distance a driver at 50 km/h covers in six or seven seconds, which is time enough to see the board, close the controller, and be sure of it. The section is placed on the 8‰ climb so that a Down train coasting through it loses a little speed and no more, and an Up train gains a little and no more.

6.2 Millers' Crossing

At km 4.700 the farm track from Millers' to the upper meadows crosses the line on the level. It is a farm crossing: a gate on each side, kept shut and opened by whoever crosses, with a cattle grid outside each gate so that stock cannot wander onto the formation when a gate is left. The formation is decked between the rails and for a metre either side with timber laid flush with the rail head, and the cess drains pass under the decking in short culverts that the ganger clears with every walk.

The crossing has no signals of its own. The line warns for it with a whistle board, a black W on an ivory disc, 200 m before the crossing in each direction, at 4.500 for Down trains and 4.900 for Up trains, at which the driver sounds one long blast (Rule R 18). Two hundred metres is the sighting distance the Engineer keeps clear of hedge and stack on either side, so that the blast and the sight of the train arrive together.

6.3 The block sections

The single line between one station and the next is a block section, and the boxes admit one train into it at a time under the Line Warrant of Book S. There are three: section A, Ashgrove to Wending, from home signal AG 2 at 0.420 to home signal WD 1 at 2.880; section B, Wending to Coldwater, from WD 8 at 3.440 to CW 1 at 5.980; and section C, Wending to Fernhollow, from WD 10 at F 0.080 to FH 1 at F 2.030. Sections B and C begin at two different homes on two different lines and meet at the junction, which belongs to neither of them: the junction and the north stub are Wending's, inside its limits, and the Box locks them for one movement at a time. To the Engineer a block section is a length of line that must be handed to the boxes clear, and taken back from them clear: no trolley, no gang and no material may stand in it without the box being told and the section being kept from trains meanwhile. The rules for that, the possession of the line for work, are for a later edition; the three lengths themselves are fixed here, home signal to home signal, so that everyone measures them alike.

6.4 The junction

The junction switch WD J at km 3.360 is the one place on the Railway where the line divides. Its toe faces Wending; lying normal it carries the main line straight on to Coldwater, lying reverse it turns a train over the branch curve towards Fernhollow. For a Down train it is a facing switch, one that chooses; for an Up train from either line it is a trailing switch, one that merely receives, and which a train run through against its setting would spring and damage. Its indicator at the toe shows which way it lies, and a driver leaving Wending for the north reads it before the switch as Book S requires, since WD 7 and WD 9 clear for either destination and the switch, not the signal, says which.

The Engineer's interest in the junction is that it wears faster than anything else on the line. Every train to or from Coldwater and every train to or from Fernhollow passes over its blades and its crossing nose, the diverging ones striking the blade at an angle, and the 25-degree curve begins at the heel of the switch, where the outer rail takes the side thrust of a train turning at 25 km/h. The switch is therefore walked with every length, gauged at the toe, the heel and the nose, and its blades are examined for the fit against the stock rail that the indicator promises and the driver trusts. It is laid on the level and kept on it: a junction on a grade would ask a stopped portion to hold itself against gravity while its other half moved away.

§ 7 Looking after the line

Track does not fail; it wears, settles and drowns, all of them slowly and all of them visibly to someone who walks it. This chapter says what to look for. The procedures for putting it right are for the next edition.

What wears

  • The rail head. Every wheel polishes it and every braking wheel heats it. On a straight line it wears evenly and slowly; at the switches, where wheels strike the switch blades, it wears fastest.
  • The ballast. It settles under traffic, and where water stands it fouls with mud until it no longer drains and the sleepers begin to pump. A wet, dark patch of ballast is the first sign.
  • The drainage. Ditches silt, culverts choke with leaves, and the water that should be in them is in the formation instead.
  • The fastenings. Clips loosen with vibration and pads wear thin. A loose clip is quiet; a hundred loose clips are a gauge fault.
  • The crossing and the gap. The decking at Millers' Crossing wears under hooves and tyres and its culverts choke; the insulators of the neutral section collect soot from every pantograph that passes and must be wiped before they track.
  • The junction and the curve. The blades and the crossing nose of WD J take every train of two lines, and the outer rail of the branch curve takes the side thrust of each one turning; the check rail through the curve wears on its inner face and is the first rail on the Railway that will need turning or renewing.

What the Engineer walks the line for

The Engineer, or the ganger for the length, walks the whole line at intervals, always from the same end so that every post is passed in the same order and nothing is missed. The walk looks at the ditches first, then at the ballast, then along the rails for dips and kinks by eye, and at the switches for the fit of the blades. The gauge is measured at the posts and at every switch. What is seen is written down against the post nearest to it, and the written record, not the memory, is what decides when work is done.

What drivers report

The people who pass over the track most often are the drivers, and a train tells its driver a great deal: a lurch, a rough ride, a sound that was not there yesterday. Such things are reported to the Box at the next stop and written in the Incident Book, not as a fault of the driver but as a note for the Engineer, and by the post nearest to them: "rough riding at 1.7", "water on the ballast at 2.3".

Rule P 14Reporting

Report anything wrong with the line to the Box by the last post passed.

Inspection intervals, the measuring of track geometry, temporary speed restrictions, possessions of the line for work, and the vehicles the Engineer uses will be issued with a later edition of this Book, once the Railway has run long enough to know what its track needs. Until then the line is walked, watched and reported on, and that is most of the work anyway.

§ 8 Glossary

Formation
The prepared ground on which the ballast and track are laid.
Ballast
The bed of broken stone that holds the sleepers, spreads the load and drains the track.
Cess
The walking strip and drain along each side of the formation.
Gradient
The slope of the line along its length, given in per mille in the Down direction.
Per mille
Metres of rise or fall in every thousand metres of line; 12‰ is 1 in 83.
Profile
The line drawn as height against distance: the gradient diagram of § 3.
Change of grade
A place where one gradient ends and another begins; marked by a gradient post.
Warning distance
The distance from an advance speed board to the speed board it announces: 200 m, or 250 m on a falling approach of 10‰ or more.
Hectometre post
A plate every 100 m giving the kilometrage; the Engineer's and the driver's reference for every position.
Kilometre post
The hectometre plate on a larger scale at every full kilometre, reading the kilometre with its zero (2.0) in the same black figures, with a green rim.
Gradient post
A post at a change of grade whose two arms give the grade on each side.
Neutral section
An insulated gap in the overhead line where two feeds meet, carrying no volts; at km 4.190–4.210, marked by section and resume boards.
Crossing
A place where a road or track crosses the line on the level; Millers' Crossing at km 4.700, warned by whistle boards.
Block section
The single line between two boxes, home signal to home signal, into which one train at a time is admitted under a Line Warrant; sections A, B and C.
Branch
A line that leaves the main line at a junction and keeps its own kilometrage; the Fernhollow branch, lettered F, from WD J to F 2.320.
Junction
The switch at which two lines divide or meet; WD J at km 3.360, facing for Down trains and trailing for Up trains.
Check rail
A rail laid inside the running rail through a curve to hold the wheels against the outer rail; on the branch curve.
