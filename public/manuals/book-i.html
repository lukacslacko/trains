§ 1 Who we are

Before the paint and the type, a word about the railway they belong to. Everything in this book follows from a survey line and a habit.

A line is only a promise until something runs along it.Valley Survey, field book 3

1.1 The line and the name

When the Valley Survey came to the Wend, it did what surveys do. It drove a stake into the meadow below Ashgrove, sighted a star, and laid out a meridian, a true north–south line, up the floor of the valley to the weir at Wending. Every field, mill and road was measured from that line. The surveyors were patient people. They checked their chains against a standard each morning and wrote everything down in a fair hand, because a survey that cannot be read is not a survey.

The first railway in the valley was built along that line, and it inherited two things from the surveyors: the name, and the habit. The name is the Meridian Railway. The habit is precision that is written down.

1.2 The motto

Our motto is On the line, on time. It is a small joke that turned into a standard. A train on the line is where it should be; a train on time is when it should be. The two together are the whole of the job, and we would rather promise the whole of the job in five words than part of it in fifty.

The motto sits on the back cover of every Book and beneath the edition line on the front. It is set in italic text face, never in capitals, and never shortened.

1.3 What the identity is for

An identity is not decoration. It is the visible half of a promise. When a passenger sees the mark on a name board at dusk, or a driver picks up a Book with the letter on its cover, they should feel two things at once: that this railway knows what it is doing, and that it is kind about it. A railway that only looks competent is cold. A railway that only looks friendly is not trusted with a level crossing.

So the identity is built to be three things, in this order.

Exact
Lines are straight, numbers are monospaced, colours are named, rules are numbered. Nothing is approximate on purpose.
Calm
Few colours, wide spacing, no exclamation marks. The page and the platform do not shout.
Warm
Ivory rather than white. Brass rather than chrome. A serif for reading. A sentence that explains why.
Rule I 10One identity

Use the identity as this Book describes it, on every vehicle, sign, document and message of the Railway. Where this Book is silent, choose the plainer option and ask the Drawing Office.

A railway is seen in pieces: a sign here, a coach there, a slip pasted in a Book. The pieces only add up to one railway if each is drawn to the same hand. Consistency is not vanity; it is how a passenger at a strange station knows they are still with us.

Rule I 12The name

Write the full name as The Meridian Railway, with the article capitalised at the start of a sentence or on a cover and lower-case elsewhere. Abbreviate only to MR, and only on plates and stock. Do not write "Meridian Rail", "Meridian Railways", "MRC" or "the MR".

"Meridian" alone is the valley's survey line, not the Railway. A passenger can stand on the meridian; they can only travel on the Meridian Railway.

2.2 The wordmark

The wordmark is the name set in a fixed way: MERIDIAN in Barlow Condensed at weight 600, upper case, with tracking of 0.22 em, and RAILWAY beneath it at roughly three-tenths of the size, weight 500, tracked to 0.56 em so that both lines are the same width. The wordmark is always centred on its own axis, which is the meridian line of the mark when the two are locked up together (§ 3.4).

§ 3 The mark

The mark is a circle bisected by a vertical line, with a short horizontal tick at the centre. The circle is the valley seen from above; the line is the meridian; the tick is the rail crossing it. That is the whole of the symbolism, and it is enough.

3.1 Construction

The mark is drawn on a square of 100 units. All strokes are 8 units wide with round ends. The circle has a radius of 42 units about the centre of the square; the meridian runs from 4 to 96 units; the tick runs from 36 to 64 units. Nothing else is added.

Rule I 14The mark

Reproduce the mark from the Drawing Office master, never redrawn by hand. Draw it in Meridian Green on Ivory, or in Ivory on Meridian Green. Where a document cannot print colour, Ink on white is permitted for that document only.

3.2 Clear space and size

Give the mark room. The clear space around it is half its own width on every side; nothing else, not even the wordmark, comes inside that space except in the lock-up of § 3.4. The smallest size at which the mark may be reproduced is 8 mm across in print and 24 pixels on a screen. Below that the tick closes up and the mark becomes a target.

Rule I 16Clear space and minimum size

Keep a clear space of half the mark's width on every side. Do not reproduce the mark smaller than 8 mm or 24 pixels across.

3.3 Right and wrong

The mark is simple enough that people are tempted to improve it. Please do not. The tiles below show the three permitted colourings and five of the commoner improvements.

Rule I 18Misuse of the mark

Do not rotate, outline, stretch, shadow, recolour or crop the mark, and do not place it on a photograph or a patterned ground. Do not use the mark as a letter in a word or as a signal aspect.

The mark is a circle with a line through it. So is a prohibition sign, a crosshair and a compass rose. Its meaning is held only by the exact proportions and the two house colours. Change either and it becomes one of the others.

3.4 The lock-up

The mark and wordmark appear together in two arrangements. In the horizontal lock-up the mark stands to the left of the wordmark, its height equal to the height of MERIDIAN plus RAILWAY together, separated by a gap equal to the tick's length. In the stacked lock-up the mark sits above the wordmark, its meridian on the wordmark's axis, separated by a gap of half the mark's width. The stacked form is for covers and name boards; the horizontal form is for letterheads, tickets and the cab side.

4.1 The colours, one by one

Meridian Green is the colour of the Railway. It is a deep green with a little blue in it, chosen because it holds its depth in rain and reads as black from a distance without ever being black. It covers vehicle bodies, Book covers, the borders of name boards and the mark. Where there is a doubt about which colour a large surface should be, it should be this one.

Ivory is our white. Pure white glares against green and looks like a repair. Ivory is the paper of the Books, the waist band on rolling stock, the field of every board and the lettering on every green surface. It is warm on purpose.

Signal Red means danger and nothing else. It is the STOP aspect, the buffer beam, the band on a Limit of Shunt board and the red face of the baton. Because it means danger, it is never used to make something look lively. A red heading is a heading that is lying.

Brass is the metal of the Railway and the colour of its small pride: the lining line along a coach, the fleet numerals, the badge on a cap, the figure numbers in the Books. It is applied thinly. A lot of brass looks like a bandstand.

Ink is for reading and for the bar on a stop board. It is not quite black, so that a page does not look burnt.

Slate is the working grey: signal posts, roofs, the lines of a diagram, a caption. It recedes, which is its job.

Chalk is the faintest colour we have. It rules tables, fills the grid under a drawing and draws the dotted leaders in a contents list. It should never carry text.

Amber and Lamp Green are glasses, not paints. They exist so that a driver never sees them anywhere but in a signal lamp, and so that when they do see them they know exactly what is meant.

Rule I 20Use of colour

Use only the nine standards. Large surfaces are Meridian Green or Ivory. Signal Red is used only where it means danger. Brass is used thinly, for lining, numerals and badges. Do not tint, blend or lighten a standard to make a new colour.

Rule I 22Lamp colours are reserved

Amber and Lamp Green are for signal aspects only. Do not use them on a vehicle, a board, a document or a screen for any other purpose.

A driver's eye is trained to react to amber and green lamps without thinking. If those colours appear on a poster beside the line, the eye reacts to the poster. We keep them out of everything else so that a lamp is always a lamp.

§ 6 Livery

Rolling stock is painted the same way whether it is a motor car, a locomotive or a coach. A train is one thing, and it should look like one thing from the platform.

  1. 1 Roof in Slate, including the pantograph well and the roof edge.
  2. 2 Ivory waist band at window level, the full length of the body between the red ends. Doors carry the band across.
  3. 3 Brass lining, one line 25 mm wide, 60 mm below the band.
  4. 4 Ends and buffer beams in Signal Red, wrapped 150 mm round on to the side.
  5. 5 Fleet number in Brass, Barlow Condensed 600, 180 mm high, below the band at both ends.
  6. 6 The mark in Ivory, 400 mm across, centred on the lower panel of the body side.
  7. 7 Class nickname in small Ivory capitals, tracked, on the lower panel beside the cab door.
  8. 8 Pantograph and roof gear in Ink, unpainted where they work.

6.1 Fleet numbering

Every vehicle carries a four-figure fleet number. The first figure is the class; the rest count up from 001, except for coaching stock, which counts from 101 so that its numbers never look like a locomotive's.

Rule I 26Livery

Paint rolling stock as Fig. 6.1: Meridian Green body, Ivory waist band, one Brass lining line, Signal Red ends and buffer beams, Slate roof, Brass fleet numerals at both ends, the mark in Ivory centred on each side. Do not add stripes, names, advertisements or a second lining line.

Rule I 28Fleet numbers

Number vehicles in the series of Fig. 6.1. Show the number at both ends of each side and on each cab front. Never reuse a number; a withdrawn vehicle takes its number with it.

The waist band is at window level so that a passenger looking for their carriage sees one continuous ivory line along the train and reads the numbers against green, not against glass. The lining is one line because two lines have to be parallel, and the Works has better things to do.

§ 7 Signage

Lineside signs are the identity at its most practical. They are read at speed, in rain, by tired people. So they are large, plain and few.

7.1 Station name boards

An Ivory board with a Meridian Green border 40 mm wide, an inner rule 6 mm wide, and the station name in Barlow Condensed 600 upper case, tracked to 0.18 em, in Meridian Green. The cap height is one-third of the board height. Boards stand on two Slate posts, the lower edge 1.4 m above the platform, and are placed at both ends of every platform and opposite the stop board.

7.2 Signal name plates

Every signal carries a plate: Ink black, white letters, the station code and the signal's number separated by a space, in Barlow Condensed 600. The plate is fixed to the post at the driver's eye level and is the only white lettering on the line.

7.3 Boards

Four fixed boards are in use, drawn here so that the Works and Book S agree. Their meanings are Book S's business; their looks are ours.

Stop board
Ivory board with an Ink border, a thick Ink bar across the upper half, the station name beneath in Barlow Condensed 600.
Limit of Shunt
Ivory board with an Ink border and a Signal Red band rising diagonally to the right; the words LIMIT OF SHUNT on an Ivory strip along the foot.
Speed board
Ivory disc 600 mm across with an Ink rim 40 mm wide and the limit in km/h in Ink, Barlow Condensed 700, figures 260 mm high.
Advance speed board
Ivory equilateral triangle, point up, 700 mm on a side, with an Ink rim 40 mm wide and the coming limit in Ink, Barlow Condensed 700, figures 220 mm high, set low in the triangle.

The two speed shapes are two tenses of the same word. The disc says now: the limit on it applies from the board. The triangle says ahead: the limit on it applies at a speed board one warning distance further on, 200 m on this line, and the driver has that distance in which to come down to it. Only reductions are announced; a higher limit gets a disc and nothing before it. An advance speed board stands on the same side of the line as the disc it announces, facing the trains that will meet them both, so that a driver reads triangle then disc, always in that order and never one without the other. In fog or snow the triangle is the one that matters, because the disc may not be seen in time.

7.4 Hectometre and kilometre posts

The line is measured from the Ashgrove buffer stop, and every hundred metres of it is named by a post. A hectometre post is an Ivory plate 320 × 180 mm on a Slate post, with the distance in Ink figures in Barlow Condensed 600, 120 mm high: the kilometre, a point, and the hectometre digit, so that the post at 1.4 km reads 1.4. At every full kilometre the plate is a kilometre post instead: Ivory, 360 × 240 mm, with a Meridian Green border 12 mm wide, the word KM in green 40 mm high, and the kilometre alone beneath it in green figures 150 mm high, so that the post at 2 km reads 2. Posts are a position reference and nothing more. They are not signals and carry no instruction; Book S says what they are for.

Posts stand on the left of the line in the Down direction, the face of the plate parallel to the track so that it reads from either direction, the top of the plate 1.2 m above rail level. Where a loop track lies on that side the post moves outward past it, so that within the loops the posts stand beyond track 2. On a platform the plate is fixed to the platform wall without a post. A post is never moved to make room for a sign; the sign moves.

Hectometre post
Ivory plate, Slate border, Ink figures: kilometre, point, hectometre digit. One every 100 m from km 0.0 to the end of the line.
Kilometre post
Ivory plate with a Meridian Green border, the word KM small above the kilometre in green figures. One at every full kilometre, in place of the hectometre post.

7.5 The baton

The station master's baton is a disc 200 mm across on a turned handle: Meridian Green on one face, Signal Red on the other, each face with an Ivory rim 12 mm wide and a small lens at the centre for the lamp. The green face shown steadily means ready to start; the red face means stop. The handle is unpainted ash, which darkens with use and is left to.

7.6 Uniform

Staff in uniform wear a Slate cap with a Meridian Green band and the MR badge in Brass at the front: a disc with the letters MR in Barlow Condensed 700 inside a thin Brass ring. Drivers and station masters wear the same badge; the station master's is the one holding the baton.

Rule I 30Signage

Make name boards, plates, boards, posts and batons to the drawings in this chapter. Hectometre and kilometre posts stand at every hundred metres and every kilometre without exception, and are the only lineside plates that carry a distance. Every speed board that lowers the limit has an advance speed board of the same figures 200 m before it; a speed board that raises the limit has none. Do not add a sign that is not in Book S, and do not vary a drawing to suit a site; move the post instead.

§ 8 Voice

The Railway writes the way a good colleague speaks: calmly, exactly, and with the courtesy of assuming you are competent.

8.1 Three habits

Calm. We do not raise our voice on paper. There is no exclamation mark anywhere in the Books except in the single word DANGER, and that word appears only where someone could be hurt. Capitals are for names of aspects (STOP, CAUTION, CLEAR) and for boards, not for emphasis.

Exact. We give the number. Not "stop close to the board" but "stop with the front within 3 m short of the board". Not "slowly" but "at 5 km/h or less over the last 20 m". Not "soon" but "06:12".

Courteous. Rules are imperative because that is the clearest form: Sound one short blast before moving. Explanations are written as we, because the reader is one of us and deserves to know why. We say what to do rather than what not to do, wherever the positive form is as clear.

Rule I 32Voice

Write rules in the imperative. Write explanations as "we". Give numbers where a number exists. Use no exclamation mark except in the word DANGER, and use that word only where a person could be hurt.

8.2 Messages from a Box

A message from a signal box to a driver is short, in full sentences, and signed with the sender's name and station. Signal names are written as on their plates, with the space. The instruction comes first, the state of the signal second, the signature last.

Draw forward to the headshunt. AG 4 cleared. — Marrow, Ashgrove
Set back on to track 2 and stop short of the Limit of Shunt. WD 6 and WD 4 cleared. — Pell, Wending
Wait. The line is not yet clear. — Marrow, Ashgrove
Rule I 34Box messages

Write a Box message as: the instruction; then the signals cleared, by name; then a dash, the sender's surname and the station. Nothing else.

A driver reads a message with one hand on the brake. The instruction is what they act on, so it comes first; the signal name is what they check against the lineside, so it comes second; the signature tells them who to answer.

9.3 Numbering and citation

Chapters are numbered § 1, § 2 and so on; sections within them § 3.2. Rules are numbered in tens within each Book, Rule S 10, Rule S 12, leaving room for a rule to be inserted between two others without renumbering the rest. Figures are numbered by chapter, Fig. 6.1. A rule is cited from any Book, including its own, by its full name: "see Rule R 20". Never "see above" and never "rule 20".

Each Book has a contents page, a running footer with the Book letter and chapter, a colophon and a back cover. Page size is A5. The running footer is set in Display, upper case, tracked, in Slate.

9.4 Editions and amendments

A Book is reissued as a new edition whenever its text or its figures change; a change to the shared stylesheet alone is not an edition. Every new edition is accompanied by an amendment slip, numbered by Book letter and edition, so that Amendment Slip I-2 takes Book I from its first edition to its second. The slip lists every change in Book order: the section, a line on why, and the wording before and after, or the new text in full. It is a small Book page in its own right, kept with the Book at amendments/book-i-ed2.html, linked from the Book's colophon and from the Library, and linking back to the sections it amends. A holder of the previous edition reads the slip, or pastes it inside the back cover, and is current; the colophon of every edition names the slips that lead to it.

Rule I 36Documents

Issue every standing instruction of the Railway as part of a lettered Book, in the house style, with a cover, contents, numbered sections and rules, a colophon and a back cover. Cite rules by Book letter and number.

Rule I 38Amendments

Change a Book only by issuing a new edition, and issue no edition without its amendment slip listing every change. Link the slip from the colophon and from the Library. Do not annotate a Book by hand except to initial a pasted slip.

A Book that everyone has, in the same edition, is a Book everyone can quote to each other over a bad telephone line. The slip is for the one who read the last edition and has five minutes: it tells them exactly what moved, and nothing else.
