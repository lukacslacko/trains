§ 1 Why a timetable

A timetable looks like a list of times. It is two other things first: a promise the Railway makes to people who are not yet on it, and the quiet half of the arrangement that keeps trains apart on a single line.

The Box keeps the trains apart in space. The timetable keeps them apart in time. We need both, and we write both down.Traffic Office, first minute book

1.1 The promise

The public timetable is the Railway's word to the valley: a train will call at Wending at eight minutes past eight, and another at eleven minutes to nine. Nobody who reads it sees a signal, a warrant or a cab. They see a time, and they arrange their morning around it. This is why a departure before the booked time is written in the Incident Book and a late one is only noted in the margin; the reasons are set out in Rule R 20 and Book R § 9, and this Book does not repeat them.

1.2 The safety

Between Ashgrove and Coldwater there is one line. Two trains cannot be on the same stretch of it at once, and the arrangements that see to this are of two kinds. The block, worked by the boxes with Line Warrants under Rule S 28, makes it impossible for a second train to be admitted to a section while the first is in it. The timetable makes it unnecessary: the trains are timed so that, on a normal day, each one reaches the loop before the other needs the section beyond it, and nobody has to be held at a home signal wondering why. The block is the wall; the timetable is the door that is always in the right place. A railway with the wall alone is safe and slow. A railway with the door alone is quick until the first misunderstanding.

1.3 Two timetables

The Railway keeps two documents from one set of figures.

Public timetable
Station, arrival, departure. Printed on the name boards and in the valley's post offices. It shows the passenger's minutes and nothing else.
Working timetable
The same trains with everything a passenger never sees: the train number, the car or the locomotive, the platform track, the dwell, the block section, the shunting moves, the recovery minutes and the crossing. Every driver and every box works from it. This Book prints it in § 6.

We keep the two apart so that the public one can be read at a glance and the working one can be trusted to the minute. A figure that appears in both is the same figure; the working timetable is never allowed to drift from the promise.

§ 2 Train numbers

A car has a number painted on its ends. A train has a number written in the timetable. They are not the same thing, and the boxes and the drivers use both, each for its own purpose.

Rule T 10Train numbers

Every train in the working timetable carries a number. Down trains carry odd numbers, Up trains even. A number belongs to one path on one day; a car or a locomotive may work several numbers in turn.

Signals already carry odd numbers when they face Down and even when they face Up (Book S § 6). We gave trains the same habit so that a person reading either the timetable or a signal plate knows the direction at once, without a second look.

2.1 The car and the train

The boxes speak of cars, because a box can see a car and cannot see a timetable: Line clear for 1002 to Wending names the vehicle that will stand in the section. The timetable speaks of trains, because a timetable is a plan and a plan is about paths, not paint. When car 1002 leaves Ashgrove at 08:00 it is train 3; when it leaves Coldwater at 08:40 it is train 6. The Incident Book, which records what happened to people and vehicles, names the car; the Traffic Office, which records how the day went against the plan, names the train.

A train that divides keeps its number on the main line and gives its branch portion a branch number: train 7 arrives at Wending as two cars and leaves it as train 7 for Coldwater and train 31 for Fernhollow. A train that is joined takes the number of the portion that stood waiting: train 32 from Fernhollow is called on to train 8 at Wending, and one train 8 leaves for Ashgrove. § 5 sets out the rules.

Where a train is more than one vehicle, the timetable names the passenger vehicle that the boxes will watch for arrival: in Duty 201 the coach, 5107, since the locomotive leaves it to run round. A train has arrived complete when its last vehicle is within station limits and it has stopped; a box cancels a warrant on nothing less.

§ 3 Running times and dwell

A booked time is a running time, a dwell and a little slack, added up and rounded to the minute in the direction of kindness. This chapter shows the arithmetic so that a driver who is a minute early knows why, and a driver who is a minute late knows it was foreseen.

3.1 Running times

The Class 1 car reaches 50 km/h in about half a minute on the level and holds it up the 12‰ without effort. Its runs, measured on the Railway with a stopwatch and a clean stop at every board, are these.

3.2 Recovery

The difference between measured and booked is the recovery allowance, and it is deliberately wide. Two minutes over Ashgrove–Wending and four over Wending–Coldwater cover a driver who brakes a little early for a distant at caution, a pantograph that takes its time, a wet rail on the climb, and fog. Recovery is not an invitation to dawdle; it is the reason a train that has been held for a moment can still arrive to time.

We would rather write a longer time and keep it than a shorter one and explain it. A timetable that can only be kept by a perfect driver on a perfect day is a promise the Railway cannot make.

3.3 Dwell

A dwell is the time a train stands at a station between its booked arrival and its booked departure. The Railway books three kinds.

Calling dwell
One to two minutes at a platform for doors: enough for the doors, the step and a passenger with a bicycle.
Crossing dwell
Four to five minutes at Wending. The first train to arrive waits for the second; the second needs a minute to arrive complete; then each needs line clear for the section the other has just left. See § 4.
Dividing and joining dwell
Seven minutes at Wending for a train that divides: the calling dwell, the uncoupling, the tail lights on the new rear cab, and two departures two minutes apart; and up to nine for a train that joins, since the standing portion must wait for the arriving one to be called on and coupled, and the brake to be proved. See § 5.
Turnround dwell
Nineteen minutes at a terminus. Changing ends means walking the length of the car and setting the lights at both; running round means five shunting moves, two walks and a brake test. These take real minutes, and we book them so that they are taken calmly.
Rule T 12Booking and line clear

A train is never booked to leave a station before the box can be given line clear for the section ahead. Where two trains share a section in turn, the second is booked to leave after the first is booked to have arrived complete, plus the minute the boxes need to speak.

This is why the two trains at Wending in Duty 301 are both booked away at 08:12 and not 08:10: train 4 is booked to arrive at 08:08, and its arrival complete is what frees section B for train 3 and lets Wending Box ask Coldwater for line clear at 08:10.

§ 4 The block and the crossing

Two trains on one line meet at a loop or not at all. This chapter says what a loop must have for a crossing to be timetabled, and what the boxes and the drivers do while it happens.

4.1 The block sections

SectionBetweenFrom home to homeWorked by
AAshgrove–Wendingkm 0.42 – 2.88Ashgrove Box and Wending Box
BWending–Coldwaterkm 3.44 – 5.98Wending Box and Coldwater Box
CWending–FernhollowF 0.08 – F 2.03Wending Box and Fernhollow Box

Sections B and C both begin at Wending's north end, and they share the north stub and the junction switch WD J. The stub and the junction are worked as one route at a time under route locking (Rule S 32): a train leaving for Coldwater, a train leaving for Fernhollow, a train arriving from either, each holds WD J from the clearing of its signal until it has passed clear, and the next may not have the junction until then. The timetable therefore never books two movements over WD J in the same minute; where two portions leave in turn, or a train arrives as another leaves, they are booked two minutes apart.

Each section has one Line Warrant. The box at the far end issues it when the section is clear and no other warrant for it is out; the box at the near end clears its starter only with the warrant in hand; the far box cancels it when the train has arrived complete. One warrant, one train, one section: the whole of Rule S 28 in a sentence. The timetable plans around it; it never plans against it.

4.2 What a crossing needs

A crossing station must have two tracks that a passenger train can use, each with a platform, each with its own starting signal in each direction that trains will leave, and a home signal at each end that can be cleared into either track. Wending has all of these: platform 1 on track 1, kept for Down trains, and platform 2 on track 2, kept for Up trains; WD 2 and WD 4 starting Up, WD 7 and WD 9 starting Down; WD 1 and WD 8 as homes, and since the branch opened a third home, WD 10, for trains arriving off it. WD 8 and WD 10 carry a subsidiary so that a portion can be called on to a platform where its other half already stands (§ 5). A loop without a second platform can pass a train but cannot cross two that both call, and the timetable would have to send one of them through without stopping.

Down trains to platform 1 and Up trains to platform 2 is a habit, not a law of nature. We keep it so that a passenger who uses Wending daily learns which side to stand, and so that the boxes' routes are the same every day and the drivers' expectations with them.

4.3 The crossing, minute by minute

Take the first crossing of Duty 301, trains 3 and 4, as the boxes and drivers see it.

  1. 07:58 Wending gives Ashgrove line clear for train 3 and issues warrant A; Ashgrove clears AG 1. Wending gives Coldwater line clear for train 4 and issues warrant B; Coldwater clears CW 2. WD 1 is cleared into track 1 and WD 8 into track 2, both at CAUTION, since both trains will stop.
  2. 08:00 Both batons are shown. Both trains leave. Each section now holds exactly one train and one warrant.
  3. 08:07 Train 3 stops at the board on platform 1. It is within station limits and stopped: Wending cancels warrant A. Section A is free again, but nobody may enter it until a box asks.
  4. 08:08 Train 4 stops on platform 2; warrant B is cancelled.
  5. 08:10 Wending asks Coldwater for line clear for train 3 and receives warrant B; WD 7 is cleared. Wending asks Ashgrove for train 4 and receives warrant A; WD 4 is cleared. The two warrants have changed hands, and each train now holds the section the other has just vacated.
  6. 08:12 Doors closed, batons shown, one short blast each, and the trains leave in opposite directions.
Rule T 14Crossing

At a crossing, the train that arrives first waits for the other. The box holds both starters until the second train has arrived complete and line clear has been given for each train's onward section. Neither train leaves before its booked time, and neither leaves on the strength of the other's warrant.

Both trains' departures at a crossing also satisfy Rule R 20 in full: doors, booked time, starting signal, baton. The warrant is the box's business; the driver sees it only as a starter that clears, and a baton that follows.

§ 5 Portions

Since the Fernhollow branch opened, a train from Ashgrove may be two cars that part company at Wending, and a train to Ashgrove may be two cars that meet there. Each half is a portion. This chapter says how the timetable counts them, when each may leave, and how the two find each other again.

5.1 Dividing

Train 7 leaves Ashgrove as one train of two cars, 1002 and 1003, coupled with pipe and control line and worked from the leading cab under Rule D 26. It is one train the whole way to Wending: one number, one warrant, one baton. At Wending it is uncoupled as Rule D 28 describes, and from that moment there are two trains standing on platform 1: train 7, which will go on to Coldwater, and train 31, which will go up the branch to Fernhollow. Each now needs its own line clear, its own warrant, its own cleared starter and its own baton; the box treats them as it would treat two trains that had arrived separately, except that they cannot both leave at once through the same starting signal.

Rule T 16Portions

A train that divides is one train until it is uncoupled; each portion then has its own departure, baton and warrant. Portions leave a station in the order they stand, the one nearest the exit first.

The order is not always the one you would guess from the destinations. On the way up the valley the Coldwater car leads and leaves first. But a pair that has turned round at Ashgrove comes back with its cars the other way about: train 9 arrives at Wending with the branch car, 1001, at the front. The branch portion, train 33, must therefore leave before the Coldwater portion can, and the timetable books 33 at 10:54 and 9 at 10:56, not the other way round. A timetable that ignored which car stands nearest the starter would book a departure the box could not give.

Two portions leaving through WD 7 in turn need the junction twice: WD J straight for Coldwater, WD J for the branch for Fernhollow. Under route locking (Rule S 32) the second route cannot be set until the first portion has passed clear of the switch, which is why the two departures are booked two minutes apart and not one.

5.2 Joining

Joining is dividing in reverse, and it is booked around the portion that arrives first. The standing portion takes its platform, arrives complete, and waits with its train brake applied; its warrant is cancelled like any other. The arriving portion is not given a clear home: it is called on under the home signal's subsidiary (Rule S 30), which tells its driver that the platform is occupied and to proceed at shunting speed prepared to stop short of the vehicles. It couples to the standing portion at 2 km/h or less, its driver connects the pipe and control line, and the brake is proved from the leading cab. The joined train then leaves under the number of the portion that stood waiting, with one warrant and one baton: train 32 from Fernhollow is called on to train 8 at platform 2, and one train 8 leaves for Ashgrove; train 34 is called on to train 10, and train 10 takes the pair home.

The standing portion is always the one booked to arrive first, and it is always given the platform kept for its direction, so that the arriving portion comes in behind it and the joined train's leading cab is the one that has been there all along. If the arriving portion is late, the standing one waits: a joined train cannot leave as half of itself.

5.3 How the timetable shows it

A train that divides has two rows under its number: one for the whole train as far as the station where it divides, and one for the portion that keeps the number beyond it; the other portion has a row of its own under its branch number. Train 7 in § 6.4 is written as 1002+1003 Ashgrove–Wending and then 1002 Wending–Coldwater, and train 31 as 1003 Wending–Fernhollow. A train that is joined has a row for each portion arriving and one row for the whole under the standing portion's number: train 8 is 1004 Coldwater–Wending, then, after train 32 has been called on, 1004+1001 Wending–Ashgrove. The word split or joins in the times column says which.

§ 7 The time–distance diagram

The Traffic Office draws every timetable before it prints it. The drawing is a graph of distance against time, and a train on it is a line: steep where it runs, level where it stands. Two trains that meet on the drawing meet on the line, and the drawing says where. Two drawings follow: Duty 301, the crossing, and Duty 401, the junction.

Down trainsUp trains
1002100310041001

Where two cars run as one train the drawing shows two lines side by side, one thick and one thin: the thick line is the car whose cab drives, the thin one is coupled behind. At a split the thin line parts from the thick and, after a dotted step up to the branch band, becomes a thick line of its own; at a join a thick line comes down the dotted step and settles beside the train it has been called on to, and turns thin. Read across the Wending band from 10:07 to 10:18 and the whole of § 5 is there: a split, a join, and three departures two minutes apart.

7.1 Reading it

Follow train 3 from the bottom left. It leaves Ashgrove at 08:00 and climbs the page as it climbs the valley; the slope of the line is its speed. At Wending the line goes level for five minutes: the crossing dwell. Then it climbs again to Coldwater, where it rests on the top band for nineteen minutes before coming back down the page as train 6.

Train 4 starts at the top at the same moment and comes down. The two lines meet in the Wending band, and only there: the circle marks the crossing. Above the band the brass line is alone in section B; below it the green line is alone in section A. That each section holds one line at a time is the whole point of the drawing, and a timetable that puts two lines on one stretch outside a station band is not sent to the printer.

The faint band across the upper section is the neutral section at posts 4.19–4.21. It is drawn so that a planner sees where a train must be coasting and does not book a stop or a start there.

§ 8 Duty sheets

A duty sheet is the driver's extract of the working timetable: the trains one crew works in one turn, with the stations, the booked times and a line for each actual time.

8.1 What is on it

The sheet lists each leg of the duty: from, booked departure, to, booked arrival, and beside them the actual times as they happen. Beneath the legs it carries the duty in brief: the preparing steps, the crossing or the run-round, and the stabling. The cab shows the sheet beside the messages from the boxes and the Incident Book, and the leg in hand is marked.

A leg over the branch is written like any other, with Fernhollow as its end; the driver reads the branch by its own posts, lettered F, and stops at the Fernhollow board at F 2.255. A driver whose train divides sees only their own portion's legs: the sheet for Duty 401 runs Ashgrove–Wending, Wending–Coldwater, Coldwater–Wending, Wending–Ashgrove, and says in brief that 1003 is uncoupled at the first Wending call and called on at the second. The colleague's branch legs are on the colleague's sheet.

8.2 Bringing the clock forward

While a train stands at a station with time in hand, the booked departure on the sheet may be used to bring the clock to fifteen seconds before it. This is a convenience of the sheet, not of the line: the world runs on through the skipped minutes, pantographs finish rising, boxes clear their signals at their usual two minutes before time, and the skip stops of itself if anything moves or anything is written in the Incident Book. Nothing about the departure itself changes.

We timed the dwells for people, not for clocks. A driver who has done everything the dwell is for should not have to watch the last nine minutes of it pass.

8.3 Early and late

A departure before the booked time is an incident under Rule R 20; a late arrival is noted on the sheet and is not. The Traffic Office reads the sheets weekly and moves the minutes, not the drivers: a run that is late every day has a timetable problem, and the cure is in this Book.

§ 9 Glossary

Working timetable
The full timetable the Railway works from: trains, vehicles, tracks, dwells, sections and shunting moves. Abbreviated WTT.
Public timetable
The passenger's extract: station, arrival, departure.
Train number
The number of a path in the working timetable; odd Down, even Up (Rule T 10). Distinct from the number painted on a car.
Path
A train's planned progress along the line in time; a line on the time–distance diagram.
Dwell
The booked time between arrival and departure at a station: calling, crossing or turnround.
Recovery
The minutes booked beyond the measured running time, so that a small delay does not become a late arrival.
Crossing
Two trains passing each other at a loop, one on each track (Rule T 14).
Block section
The single line between two boxes, from home signal to home signal; sections A and B on the main line, C on the branch.
Portion
One of the parts of a train that divides at a station, or that is joined there; each portion is a train in its own right from the uncoupling to the coupling (Rule T 16).
Branch train
A train between Wending and Fernhollow, numbered in the 31–39 block; a branch portion of a main-line train becomes one when it is uncoupled.
Junction
The switch WD J beyond Wending's north stub, where the Fernhollow branch leaves the main line; worked as one route at a time (Rule S 32).
Line Warrant
The authority for one train to occupy one block section, issued and cancelled by the boxes (Rule S 28).
Arrived complete
A train wholly within station limits and stopped; the moment a warrant may be cancelled.
Time–distance diagram
The Traffic Office's drawing of distance against time on which every train is a line and every crossing a meeting.
Duty sheet
The driver's extract of the working timetable for one turn of duty.
